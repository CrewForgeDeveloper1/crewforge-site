How to calculate load weight and centre of gravity for a lift

Before any lift, you need two numbers: how much the load weighs and where its centre of gravity is. Get the weight wrong and every sling, shackle and hoist is sized wrong. Get the centre of gravity wrong and the load tips, or one leg takes far more than you planned.

Weigh it with a tape measure

Most steel you lift has no scale ticket, so you measure it. Weight is volume times density, and a cubic foot of steel weighs about 490 lb (7,850 kg/m³ in metric). Take a steel plate 6 ft by 4 ft, 2 in thick. Put every dimension in feet first: 2 in is 2/12 of a foot, so the volume is 6 × 4 × 2/12 = 4 ft³, and the plate weighs 4 × 490 = 1,960 lb. Cut the thickness to 1 in and you halve both: 980 lb. A quick check for plate: 1 in plate weighs about 40.8 lb per square foot.

Round bar, pipe and beams

Round shapes trip people up. For a solid round bar the end is a circle, so the volume is π/4 × d² × length. A bar 6 in across and 12 ft long is π/4 × 0.5² × 12 = 2.36 ft³, about 1,155 lb.

For pipe, the steel is only the ring. Find the inside diameter first by taking the wall off both sides: a pipe 8⅝ in outside with a ½ in wall is 7⅝ in inside. The ring area is π/4 × (OD² − ID²); times the length and 490 lb/ft³, a 20 ft length comes to about 869 lb. A pipe table lists that size at about 43.4 lb per foot, the same answer within a pound or two. For wide-flange beams the name gives the weight: a W8×31 weighs 31 lb per foot.

  • Plate, block: V = L × W × t (1 in plate ≈ 40.8 lb/ft²)
  • Round bar: V = π/4 × d² × L
  • Pipe: V = π/4 × (OD² − ID²) × L, ID = OD − 2 × wall
  • W-beam: weight per foot is in the name (W8×31 = 31 lb/ft)
  • Metric: lengths in metres × 7,850 kg/m³

Keep the centre of gravity between the picks

Weight is only half the job. Put a 6,000 lb motor at one end of a skid with the lifting lugs welded 4 ft in from that end, and lift it with two hoists. The centre of gravity is outside the picks, so to hold the skid level the far hoist would have to push down. A hoist, a sling or a chain can only pull, so the load tips toward the motor. Find the centre of gravity before you lift, and keep it between your picks.

Finding the centre of gravity

Multiply each part's weight by its distance from one end, add them up and divide by the total weight. A bare 12 ft skid with a 2 in deck weighs 3,920 lb and has its centre of gravity in the middle, 6 ft from the left end. Set a 3,000 lb motor on it 9 ft from the left end: (3,920 × 6 + 3,000 × 9) ÷ 6,920 = 7.3 ft. Measure every distance from the same end, and expect the answer to land closer to the heavier part.

Leg loads when the picks aren't even

A steel block 6 × 3 × 2 ft is 36 ft³, or 17,640 lb, with its centre of gravity 3 ft from each end. Put the picks 1 ft in on the left and 2 ft in on the right, and the centre of gravity sits 2 ft from the left pick and 1 ft from the right pick. The left hoist carries 5,880 lb and the right carries 11,760 lb: the pick closer to the centre of gravity carries more. Take moments about one pick to see why: 5,880 × 2 = 11,760 × 1. Each vertical leg carries the weight times the distance to the other pick, divided by the distance between the picks.

One hook, two slings

Most lifts use one hook and two slings. Put the hook right over the centre of gravity and the load hangs level. On the skid with the motor, the vertical shares are 2,560 lb and 4,360 lb, but the legs are on an angle, so each carries more than its share: the share times the leg length, divided by the hook height. The shorter, steeper right leg carries 5,122 lb and the left leg 3,712 lb. Size the slings for the heavier leg, not for half the load.

If the hook isn't over the centre of gravity, the load turns until it is. With two equal slings and the hook over the middle of the picks, the skid hangs 13° out of level and the low-side leg carries 5,608 lb. If a load tilts more than about 5°, land it and re-rig. A wire rope sling is built to break at about five times its rating, and that margin is not spare capacity: it covers wear, shock loads and small mistakes.

The formula

x̄ = (W₁ × x₁ + W₂ × x₂ + …) ÷ W
W = W₁ + W₂ + …
Measure every x from the same end
The CG sits closer to the heavier part

The centre of gravity, measured from one end, is the sum of each weight times its distance from that end, divided by the total weight. With two vertical hoists, each one carries the weight times the distance to the other pick, divided by the distance between the picks: R1 = W × b ÷ (a + b).

Worked exam questions

These questions are from the video. Try each one before you read the solution.

WORKED EXAMPLE 1

A 12 ft skid weighs 1,960 lb with its CG at the middle. A 3,920 lb motor sits with its CG 9 ft from the left end. Where is the combined CG, measured from the left end?

  • A. 6.00 ft
  • B. 7.50 ft
  • C. 8.00 ft
  • D. 9.00 ft

Answer: C. 8.00 ft

Skid, 1,960 times 6. Motor, 3,920 times 9. Add them. 47,040. Divide by the total, 5,880. 8 ft from the left end.

x̄ = (1,960 × 6 + 3,920 × 9) ÷ 5,880 = 47,040 ÷ 5,880 = 8.00 ft

B is halfway between 6 and 9, which ignores the weights. The motor is twice as heavy, so the centre of gravity sits twice as close to it.

WORKED EXAMPLE 2

A 10 ft skid deck weighs 2,450 lb with its CG at the middle. A 1,050 lb motor sits 9 ft from the left end. Picks are 1 ft in from each end, on two vertical hoists. What does the right hoist carry?

  • A. 1,225 lb
  • B. 1,750 lb
  • C. 2,275 lb
  • D. 3,500 lb

Answer: C. 2,275 lb

Take moments about the left pick. The deck's weight acts 4 ft from it. The motor is 8 ft from it. The right pick is 8 ft away too. 2,450 times 4, plus 1,050 times 8, is 18,200. Divide by 8. 2,275 lb.

Moments about the left pick: R2 × 8 = 2,450 × 4 + 1,050 × 8 = 18,200; R2 = 2,275 lb (R1 = 1,225 lb)

B splits the load in half. A is the left hoist. The right hoist is nearer the motor, so it carries more.

Common mistakes

  • Inches or millimetres not converted before multiplying
  • Pipe: solid OD, or the wall taken off once
  • CG halfway between parts, ignoring weight
  • Half the load per leg when the picks aren't even
  • Forgetting the angle: tension > vertical share

Convert inches to feet (or millimetres to metres) before you multiply, and measure every distance from the same end.
