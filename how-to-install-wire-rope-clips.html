Wire rope clips: turn-back, spacing, torque and the seven steps

Put two clips and six inches of turn-back on a 3/4 in rope and the eye can pull out under its own rating, even with the saddles on the right side and the nuts at full torque. Millwrights make and check clip eyes, and the Red Seal exam asks how many clips, how much turn-back, where each clip goes and how much torque. Follow the sheet that came with the clips, and check your province's rules.

Read one row of the table

Crosby's instruction sheet for its G-450 U-bolt clips gives each rope size a row: the minimum number of clips, the turn-back and the torque. For 3/4 in rope it's 4 clips, 18 in and 130 ft·lb. For 7/8 in it's 4 clips, 19 in and 225 ft·lb. Turn-back is measured from the thimble to the end of the dead end, before any clips go on. Read straight across one row: taking the clips from one row and the torque from another is the classic exam trap.

Three more rules from the same sheet. If you use more clips than the table, increase the turn-back in proportion. If a pulley takes the place of the thimble, add one clip, so a 5/8 in rope that takes 3 clips gets 4. And the counts assume ordinary 6 × 19 or 6 × 37 rope; some constructions need one more. Other clip types, like double saddle fist grips, have their own table. Never mix the two.

Every line of the table has to be met. Made to the table, a 3/4 in eye is rated 9,408 lb. With only two clips and 6 in of turn-back, the rope slides through and the eye pulls out under 9,000 lb. Torque can't make up for missing clips or a short tail.

The seven steps

On 1/2 in rope: turn back 11-1/2 in from the thimble. Put the first clip one base width from the dead end, U-bolt over the dead end and the live end in the saddle, and torque its nuts evenly, back and forth. Put the next clip as close to the thimble as you can, nuts firm but not tight. Space any others equally between those two. Take up the slack, then torque every nut to 65 ft·lb, alternating from one nut to the other on each clip.

Steps six and seven come after the eye is made. Apply the first load, at least as heavy as the working load, then torque every nut again to the table value with a torque wrench. The usual explanation in trade training is that the rope stretches and gets a little thinner, so the clips ease off. Keep checking at every inspection. A guy line rigged in the afternoon and loaded by wind overnight can take a quarter turn on each nut the next morning. Crosby also discourages reusing clips.

Torque: the pull on the wrench

Torque is force times the length of the wrench, so the pull is torque divided by length. Change foot pounds to inch pounds first. For 95 ft·lb on a 15 in wrench: 95 × 12 ÷ 15 = 76 lb. A 24 in wrench needs about 48 lb for the same torque. Turn it around too: 30 lb on an 18 in wrench is 540 in·lb, or 45 ft·lb, the 3/8 in row. In metric, 130 ft·lb is about 176 N·m, and on a 0.5 m wrench that's about 353 N.

Crosby's torque values are for clean, dry threads with no lubricant. Oil the threads and the same reading squeezes the rope much harder. Use a torque wrench, not a guess or an impact gun.

Spacing, lap splices and the rules

Done the maker's way, the clips on 1/2 in rope land about 4-5/16 in apart. British Columbia's OHS Regulation has its own Table 15-2 (3 clips, 76 mm apart, about 3 in, on 1/2 in rope), but s. 15.22 says to use it only when the maker's specifications aren't available. The tables don't always agree: on 1 in rope the BC table says 4 clips, Crosby says 5. Alberta's OHS Code (s. 300) puts the U-bolt on the dead end and sends you to the maker's torque, then to its own schedule if there is none. A common trade rule of thumb is about six rope diameters between clips.

To join two ropes with a clip lap splice, Crosby uses twice the clips and overlaps the ends by twice the turn-back: for 1/2 in rope, 6 clips and 23 in. BC's regulation also doubles the clips for a lap splice. Crosby prefers two eyes with thimbles, linked together.

The formula

F = T ÷ L · clean, dry threads
One row: clips, turn-back, torque
First clip a base width from the dead end
Next at the thimble, others equal
Load it, then re-torque

Pull on the wrench = torque ÷ wrench length. Change ft·lb to in·lb first: 225 ft·lb × 12 = 2,700 in·lb, ÷ 18 in = 150 lb. The table torque is for clean, dry threads.

Worked exam questions

These questions are from the video. Try each one before you read the solution.

WORKED EXAMPLE 1

A 1 in rope eye needs 225 ft·lb on each nut. Your torque wrench is 18 in long. How hard do you pull on the handle?

  • A. 12.5 lb
  • B. 150 lb
  • C. 225 lb
  • D. 4,050 lb

Answer: B. 150 lb

Force is torque divided by length. Change the foot pounds to inch pounds first. 225 times 12 is 2,700 in pounds. Divided by 18 in, 150 lb.

F = T ÷ L = 225 ft·lb × 12 in/ft ÷ 18 in = 150 lb

A divided by 18 without changing units. D multiplied instead of divided.

WORKED EXAMPLE 2

A 5/8 in rope is clipped back around a pulley (sheave) instead of a thimble. How many Crosby clips does it need?

  • A. 2
  • B. 3
  • C. 4
  • D. 6

Answer: C. 4

The five eighths row says three. Crosby says to add one clip when a pulley takes the place of the thimble. That's four.

5/8 in row: 3 clips; a pulley in place of the thimble adds one → 4

B is the plain table value. D doubled it, which is the rule for a lap splice, not a pulley.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing two rows of the table
  • Torquing by feel, or with an impact gun
  • Oil on the threads
  • Skipping the re-torque after the first load
  • Using the BC table when the maker's sheet is there

Read clips, turn-back and torque from one row, change ft·lb to in·lb before you divide by the wrench length, add a clip for a pulley, and re-torque after the first load.
