Mobile crane setup: outriggers, ground bearing pressure, mats and level

A crane can be well inside its load chart and still go over when an outrigger punches through the ground. Every chart assumes a firm, level surface under every outrigger; if the ground gives way, the chart means nothing. Millwrights work beside cranes on plant floors, gravel pads and backfilled trenches, and the Red Seal exam asks how outrigger loads, mats and soil fit together. The crane numbers here come from one maker's product guide, for training only. Rules differ by province, so check yours.

Where the weight goes

A Grove RT540E, a 40 ton rough terrain crane, weighs 60,126 lb on its own, and every pound goes down through four floats. Lift 15,000 lb and the total is 75,326 lb, but it doesn't split evenly: the load pushes its own side down and lifts the other. In our model the two load-side floats jump to about 31,000 lb each and the far side drops under 7,000 lb. Swing over a corner and one float takes about 39,400 lb. On a real job the maker publishes outrigger loads for each set-up; this crane's guide limits one pad to 58,000 lb. Use the maker's numbers.

To work out the load-side pair yourself, take moments about the far outrigger line. With 82,680 lb of crane and load, a moment toward the load of 341,430 lb·ft, and outrigger lines 11 ft each side of centre: (82,680 × 11 + 341,430) ÷ 22 = 56,860 lb on the pair, 28,430 lb on one float.

Bearing pressure and mats

Bearing pressure is force divided by area. The RT540E float is 14-1/4 in square, 1.41 ft². With 31,100 lb on it, that's about 22,000 psf, roughly seven times the 3,100 psf allowed on dense sand or gravel. A 4 × 4 ft mat is 16 ft²: 31,100 ÷ 16 = 1,944 psf, 62 % of that allowable. Divide by the area, not the side.

To size a mat, the area needed is the force divided by the allowable pressure. On firm clay, allowed 1,566 psf, 31,100 lb needs 19.9 ft², so a 4 × 4 mat (124 %) is too small and a 5 × 5 mat (1,244 psf, 79 %) works. Swing over the corner, though, and that float climbs to 39,400 lb and 1,576 psf, just over. Size mats for the worst position in the lift, not the start. And the mat must be stiff enough to spread the load; a thin, soft mat bends and the float punches through the middle anyway.

On soft clay, allowed about 835 psf, a crane on 3 ft mats already presses over 2,000 psf before the lift. Lift 15,000 lb at 73 % of the chart and the heaviest float carries about 31,000 lb, four times what the clay is allowed. The float sinks, the crane leans, the radius grows and over it goes. The chart is fine; the ground isn't.

What the ground can carry

The BC Building Code (Table 9.4.4.1) gives allowable bearing pressures: sound rock 500 kPa, clay shale 300, till 200, dense or compact sand or gravel 150, stiff clay 150, dense silt 100, firm clay 75, loose sand or gravel 50 and soft clay 40. If the water table is close below sand, gravel or silt, the code takes half the table value, so dense sand drops to 75 kPa. Multiply kilopascals by about 20.9 for pounds per square foot. These values are for small footings, a good yardstick. For a real crane setup, use the site's ground data or an engineer's number.

The regulations agree on the basics. Ontario wants outriggers extended as the load chart requires and set only after the operator has considered ground bearing pressure (O. Reg. 213/91, s. 156). BC wants the beams marked to show full extension, floats secured to the jacks, and a qualified person to pick the spot next to an excavation, slope or backfill. Alberta asks for floats or pads of adequate size, strength and rigidity. A backfilled trench looks solid and isn't.

Level

If the crane leans toward the load, the boom tip moves out by its height times the sine of the angle. With the tip about 47 ft up, one degree out of level moves it about 0.83 ft, so a 27 ft radius becomes about 27.8 ft: a smaller chart number and a side-loaded boom. Many charts say the crane must be level within 1 %, about half a degree, and even that adds about half a foot here. On a taller boom it's more. Check the level indicator before the lift, and again after the outriggers take the load.

The formula

Pressure = force ÷ bearing area
Float loads: the load side carries most; over a corner one float takes the most
BC Building Code 9.4.4.1: what soil and rock can carry
Mat area = force ÷ allowable; stiff mats
Level within the maker's limit: 1° ≈ height × 0.0175

Bearing pressure is the float load over the area it sits on. A 2 × 2 ft float with 28,400 lb: 28,400 ÷ 4 ft² = 7,100 psf. Flip it to size a mat: area needed = force ÷ allowable, then pick the next square that's bigger.

Worked exam questions

These questions are from the video. Try each one before you read the solution.

WORKED EXAMPLE 1

An outrigger float 2 ft × 2 ft carries 28,400 lb. What is the bearing pressure on the ground under it?

  • A. 14,200 psf
  • B. 7,100 psf
  • C. 113,600 psf
  • D. 1,775 psf

Answer: B. 7,100 psf

First the area. 2 times 2, 4 ft². Pressure is force over area.

Area 2 × 2 = 4 ft²; pressure = 28,400 ÷ 4 = 7,100 psf

7,100 lb/ft². It's B. A divides by 2, the side, not the area. C multiplies. D divides by 16, the area of a 4 ft mat.

WORKED EXAMPLE 2

An outrigger float carries 28,430 lb. The ground is firm clay, allowable 1,566 psf. Which is the smallest square mat that keeps the pressure within the allowable?

  • A. 3 ft × 3 ft
  • B. 4 ft × 4 ft
  • C. 5 ft × 5 ft
  • D. 8 ft × 8 ft

Answer: C. 5 ft × 5 ft

Area needed is force over allowable. 18.15 ft². 4 by 4 is only 16. 5 by 5 is 25.

Area needed 28,430 ÷ 1,566 = 18.15 ft²; 4 × 4 = 16 (too small); 5 × 5 = 25 ✓

B looks close, but it's under the area. D works too, but it's not the smallest.

Common mistakes

  • Pressure ÷ side instead of ÷ area
  • Kilograms used as if they were newtons
  • Trusting backfill or wet sand
  • A mat too thin to spread the load
  • Skipping the level check

Divide by the area, not the side, turn kilograms into newtons in metric, size the mat for the worst swing position, and check the level after the outriggers take the load.
