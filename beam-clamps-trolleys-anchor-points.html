Beam clamps, trolleys and anchor points: flange fit, side pull and anchor loads

A hoist is only as good as what it hangs from: a beam clamp, a trolley, an eye bolt or an anchor in the ceiling. Drag a load sideways with a hoist on a beam clamp and the clamp can swing off the flange. The Red Seal exam asks how to pick these supports and how much load they really see. Rules differ by province, so check yours.

Beam clamps: fit the flange, pull straight

A beam clamp grips the edges of the bottom flange with two jaws. You turn the screw until the jaws close on the flange, then check the lock nut. Every clamp has a flange range. A 2 ton Harrington clamp fits flanges 3 to 7-1/2 in wide and up to 0.83 in thick, so a 6 in flange 0.4 in thick fits. An 8 in or 10 in flange doesn't: the jaws can't reach around the edges, and no tightening fixes that. The 3 and 5 ton sizes fit 4 to 12 in.

No side pull. If a 1 ton load sits off to the side and the hoist drags it over, the chain leans off plumb and the pull splits in two. The sideways part is the chain pull times the sine of the angle, about 1,147 lb at 35°. The line of pull passes outside the far jaw, the clamp swings on that jaw, the other jaw lets go and the rig comes down. The hoist, the clamp and the load hang in one straight line. Some clamps are built for angled loads, with their own lower ratings; unless your clamp's manual says so, yours isn't.

The clamp and the hoist each have a rating, and the lower one rules. A 1 ton clamp under a 2 ton hoist makes a 1 ton rig. In BC (OHS Regulation s. 14.11) a hoist's rating can't be more than the rating of the structure holding it up, and the beam is part of that structure.

Trolleys, wheel loads and end stops

A trolley rides on the bottom flange with wheels on each side of the web. A push trolley, moved by pushing the load, suits longer runs. A geared trolley, with its own hand chain, gives precise control over shorter runs. A 2 ton trolley fits flanges 3.23 to 6.02 in, with spacer washers set to the actual beam. Four wheels share the load: 3,000 lb over four wheels is 750 lb on each wheel, a little more with the hoist and trolley weight.

Each wheel load bends the flange tip down like a weight on the end of a short shelf, and the stress goes up with one over the flange thickness squared: halve the thickness and the stress goes up four times. Overload a light beam and the flange tips bend, the wheels roll off the edges and the trolley drops through. A bigger trolley is not the fix. Whether a beam can carry a hoist is an engineer's call. And every beam needs end stops at both ends, checked before use; without them a trolley rolls off the end. BC requires end stops on hoist tracks and rails, and Ontario and Alberta require stops on crane runways and tracks.

Snatch block anchors: the sum that catches people

Both lines through a snatch block carry the same pull. With the lead line running straight back down beside the load line, the anchor holds both: twice the line pull, so 1,500 lb on the line puts 3,000 lb on the anchor. Spread the lines and the anchor load is two times the line pull times the cosine of half the angle between them. At 60° that's 2 × 1,500 × cos 30°, about 2,600 lb (Crosby's factor is 1.73). At 90° the factor is 1.41, and at 120° it's exactly 1, the same as the line pull. The block hangs halfway between the two lines, so the anchor is pulled at an angle too.

Eye bolts, beams and concrete anchors

A 3/4 in shoulder eye bolt is rated 7,200 lb in line with the bolt. Pulled off its axis it drops, from Crosby's table: 80 % at 15°, 65 % at 30°, 30 % at 45° and 25 % at 90°. At 45° it's good for only 2,160 lb. A 1/2 in bolt, 2,600 lb in line, is good for 780 lb at 45°. Crosby says never use a plain eye bolt, with no shoulder, for an angled lift.

A sling around a steel beam makes a handy anchor only if someone knows what the beam can carry. A beam that holds up a roof was designed for the roof, so get an engineer to rate it, put softeners on every edge the sling touches, and remember the rating is for the direction the engineer checked: a sideways pull can roll an I-beam. An anchor set in concrete likewise has only the capacity an engineer gives it, for that concrete, that edge distance and that direction. Overload it and the concrete breaks out in a cone around the anchor.

The formula

Anchor load R = 2 × T × cos(θ ÷ 2)
System rating = the lowest-rated part
Flange inside the range; no side pull
Wheel load = load ÷ 4 wheels (load only)
Eye bolt: WLL × the maker's angle factor
Unknown capacity → engineer

The anchor load is two times the line pull times the cosine of half the angle between the lines. For 1,500 lb at 60°: 2 × 1,500 × 0.866 = 2,598 lb, about 2,600 lb. Lines side by side (0°) double the pull; at 120° the anchor carries just the line pull.

Worked exam questions

These questions are from the video. Try each one before you read the solution.

WORKED EXAMPLE 1

A snatch block redirects a tugger line. The angle between the lead line and the load line is 60°. The line pull is 1,500 lb. About how much load does the block put on its anchor?

  • A. 1,500 lb
  • B. 2,600 lb
  • C. 3,000 lb
  • D. 750 lb

Answer: B. 2,600 lb

2 times the line pull, times the cosine of half the angle. Half of 60 is 30. The cosine of 30° is 0.866. 2 times 1,500 times 0.866 is about 2,600 lb. The maker's table gives the same factor, 1.73.

R = 2 × 1,500 × cos 30° = 2,598 lb ≈ 2,600 lb (Crosby factor 1.73 at 60°)

C treats the lines as if they were side by side. A forgets the anchor holds both lines. And D halves the pull instead of adding it.

WORKED EXAMPLE 2

A 3/4 in shoulder eye bolt has a working load limit of 7,200 lb in line. It will be loaded 45° off its axis. Using the maker's table (30 % at 45°), what is its adjusted working load limit?

  • A. 7,200 lb
  • B. 5,040 lb
  • C. 2,160 lb
  • D. 1,800 lb

Answer: C. 2,160 lb

7,200 times 30 % is 2,160 lb.

7,200 × 30 % = 2,160 lb

A ignores the angle. B takes 70 %, as if you were knocking 30 % off instead of keeping 30 %. And D is the 25 % row, for a pull at 90°.

Common mistakes

  • Line pull used as the anchor load
  • Forgetting that lines side by side double it
  • Full in-line WLL at an angle
  • Plain eye bolt on an angled pull
  • Clamp outside its flange range
  • No end stops; beam capacity assumed

Never use the line pull as the anchor load, double it when the lines run side by side, derate eye bolts by the maker's angle factor, and get an engineer for any beam or anchor nobody has rated.
