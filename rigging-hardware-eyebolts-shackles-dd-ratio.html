Rigging hardware: eyebolt angles, shackle side loads and the D/d ratio

Every piece of rigging hardware has a working load limit (WLL) stamped on it, but that number only holds for a straight pull. Pull an eyebolt sideways, load a shackle on its side, or bend a wire rope around a small pin, and the real limit drops.

The WLL is for a straight, in-line pull

A shoulder eyebolt rated 5,200 lb is allowed to carry 5,200 lb in a straight, in-line pull. Hang 4,000 lb on it straight up and it is at 77 % of its rating. The maker builds an eyebolt to break at about five times its WLL, but that margin is not yours to use: it covers wear, shock loads and the things you didn't see. Plan to the WLL, nothing more.

Shoulder eyebolts at an angle

Pull an eyebolt at an angle and the pull splits into a part straight up the bolt and a part sideways. The sideways part bends the eyebolt at the shoulder, so the maker's table cuts the allowed load. For the same 5,200 lb eyebolt: at 15° from in-line, 80 %, or 4,160 lb; at 30°, 65 %, or 3,380 lb; at 45°, only 30 %, or 1,560 lb. The eyebolt has lost more than two-thirds of its rating, and past 45° the table allows a quarter.

Two rules decide most exam questions. Measure the angle from in-line (the eyebolt's own axis), not from horizontal. And when your angle falls between two rows, use the next angle up, which is the lower percentage. A sling leg 60° from horizontal is 30° from in-line on a vertical eyebolt.

Plain eyebolts and swivel hoist rings

  • Plain eyebolt: in-line only
  • Shoulder eyebolt: seated flush, pull in the plane of the eye
  • Between table angles: use the next angle up (lower %)
  • Any angle: swivel hoist ring, per the maker's rating and torque

A plain eyebolt has no shoulder to take the sideways part of the pull, so it has no angle rating at all: straight pulls only. A shoulder eyebolt must be seated flush with the pull in the plane of the eye, never across it. A swivel hoist ring turns and pivots to line up with the pull, so makers rate many of them for their full WLL at any angle. You still check the tag and torque the bolt to the maker's figure.

Shackle side loading

A threaded-pin shackle rated 9,500 lb carries its full rating when loaded in line, through the bow and the pin. Load it from the side, in the plane of the bow, and the maker's table reduces it: at 45°, 70 %, or 6,650 lb; at 90°, square to its line, half, or 4,750 lb. Side loads go in the plane of the bow, never across it. A round-pin shackle, held only by a cotter pin, should never be side-loaded.

Shackle pin types and shapes

  • Threaded pin: quick on and off; check the pin is fully seated
  • Bolt type (bolt, nut, cotter): long-term, or where the pin could turn
  • Round pin (cotter only): never side-load
  • Bow (anchor) for more than one leg · chain (D) for in-line pulls

A threaded pin is quick on and off, but it can back out if the load rolls it. A bolt-type shackle (bolt, nut and cotter) is for long-term rigging, or where the load could slide along the pin and turn it. A bow or anchor shackle has room for more than one sling leg; a chain (D) shackle is meant for a straight, in-line pull.

The D/d ratio: wire rope bent over a pin

When a wire rope bends around a pin, a shackle or the load, the outside wires stretch and the inside wires crush. The sharper the bend, the weaker the rope. The D/d ratio measures the bend: big D is the diameter it bends around, little d is the rope's diameter. A ½ in rope around a 10 in pin is 20 : 1 and keeps 91 % of its strength. Around a 4 in pin, 8 : 1, it keeps 83 %. Around a ½ in pin, the same size as the rope, 1 : 1, it keeps only half: a sling rated 5,200 lb is good for 2,600 lb.

Find the ratio in the maker's table. If it falls between two rows, use the lower ratio, which gives the lower percentage, and multiply the rating by that percentage. On the job, use a shackle or pin big enough for the rope, and pad sharp corners.

The formula

Adjusted WLL = rating × % from the table
D/d = pin or load diameter ÷ rope diameter
Between rows: use the lower ratio (lower %)
1 : 1 = 50 % · 8 : 1 = 83 % · 20 : 1 = 91 %

For any angled or bent use, the adjusted working load limit is the tag rating times the percentage from the maker's table for that angle or D/d ratio. When your value falls between two rows of the table, use the row that gives the lower percentage.

Worked exam questions

These questions are from the video. Try each one before you read the solution.

WORKED EXAMPLE 1

A shoulder eyebolt rated 2,500 kg in-line is pulled at 40° from in-line. The table lists 30° = 65 % and 45° = 30 %. What is its adjusted WLL?

  • A. 625 kg
  • B. 750 kg
  • C. 1,625 kg
  • D. 2,500 kg

Answer: B. 750 kg

40° isn't on the table. Use the next angle up, 45. That's 30 %. 2,500 times 0.3. 750 kg.

40° is between rows: use the next angle up, 45° = 30 %. 2,500 × 0.30 = 750 kg

C used the 30° row, which allows more than the eyebolt can take at 40°.

WORKED EXAMPLE 2

Two shoulder eyebolts, each rated 2,600 lb in-line, take a 3,000 lb load on a two-leg bridle. Each leg is 60° from horizontal (LAF 1.155). Maker's table: 30° = 65 %. Will they hold?

  • A. Yes: 1,500 lb on each 2,600 lb eyebolt
  • B. Yes: 1,733 lb on each 2,600 lb eyebolt
  • C. No: 1,733 lb on a 1,690 lb adjusted WLL
  • D. No: 3,000 lb on each eyebolt

Answer: C. No: 1,733 lb on a 1,690 lb adjusted WLL

Two steps. First the leg tension. Half the load, 1,500, times the load angle factor of 1.155. About 1,733 lb per leg. Now the eyebolt. A leg 60° from horizontal is 30° from the eyebolt's in line. 30° is 65 %. 2,600 times 0.65 is 1,690. 1,733 on a 1,690 limit.

Leg tension = 3,000 ÷ 2 × 1.155 = 1,733 lb. 60° from horizontal = 30° from in-line → 2,600 × 0.65 = 1,690 lb. 1,733 > 1,690: overloaded

They're overloaded. A and B forgot the angle on the eyebolt. Fix it with longer slings, a lifting beam, or swivel hoist rings.

Common mistakes

  • Tag rating used for an angled pull
  • Plain eyebolt used at an angle
  • Eyebolt angle measured from horizontal, not in-line
  • Between rows: picking the bigger %
  • Ignoring D/d around a small pin

Read the angle from in-line, use the more conservative row, and check every part of the rigging for the angle it really sees.
