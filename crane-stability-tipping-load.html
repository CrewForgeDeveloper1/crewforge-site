Crane stability and tipping: tipping lines, the 85 % rule, deflection and swinging loads

A crane can be lifting at 80 % of its chart and still tip. A mobile crane stays up because its own weight, working on its side of the outriggers, beats the load working on the other. Millwrights rig, guide and land the loads, so you need to know what grows the radius. The Red Seal exam asks about load charts, the tipping percentages behind them and the radius you actually lift at. The crane numbers here come from one maker's product guide, for training only.

The tipping line and the 85 % rule

A crane on outriggers tips over the line through two of its floats. With the boom over the side, it's the line through the two load-side floats; on the Grove RT540E in our model that line is 11.7 ft from the centre of rotation. Seen from the side, it's the fulcrum: the crane's weight holds on the near side and the load tips on the far side.

The tipping load is the load that just balances the crane. At 40 ft of radius, worked back from the maker's chart, it's about 17,000 lb. On outriggers, maker charts rate the crane at no more than 85 % of tipping: 0.85 × 17,026 = 14,470 lb, and the chart says 14,450. On rubber, charts often use 75 %. Boom down and the tipping load falls fast: at 45 ft it's about 13,700 lb and the chart is 11,650. The 15 % between the chart and tipping isn't spare capacity. It covers deflection, swing, wind and ground.

Where the boom points changes the chart

With the outrigger beams out only 50 %, 14 ft from float to float, the side tipping line is only 7 ft out. In our model 5,973 lb gross at 30 ft over the side would tip the crane at about 15,200 lb. Swing over the front, where the floats are still 12 ft out, and the tipping load jumps to about 36,000 lb. That's why a 360° chart is set by its weakest direction, and why some cranes carry a separate over-the-front chart. Lift only in a working area that has a rating, and read the chart for that area. A lift of 12,800 lb at 39 ft is 64 % of a 20,000 lb over-the-side rating but 158 % of an 8,100 lb over-the-front rating.

Read the chart at the loaded radius

Radius is measured from the centre of rotation to the hoist line. When the load comes on, a telescopic boom bends a little and the tip moves out, which is why this maker's chart lists the loaded boom angle beside each number. If the loaded radius goes past a row, read the next greater radius; never average between rows. At 39 ft before loading plus 1.4 ft of deflection, the radius is 40.4 ft, so with 20,000 lb at 40 ft and 13,500 lb at 50 ft you use 13,500 lb.

Every chart mixes two limits. In the plain cells, stability governs: 85 % of tipping. In the shaded cells above the bold line, boom strength governs, usually close in at short radius. There the crane won't feel light before the boom fails, so you get no warning from the outriggers. And every cell assumes the crane is level, on firm ground, with the load hanging freely; makers tell you to reduce the load for wind, side loads, swinging loads and jerky stops.

A swinging load is a load at a bigger radius

Take 11,623 lb gross at 40 ft, 80 % of the chart. A fast swing and a hard stop start the load swinging. The line from the head sheave to the load is about 41.5 ft, a pendulum. Swing out 8° and the load goes out 41.5 × sin 8°, about 5.8 ft, to a radius of about 45.8 ft. Out there the tipping load is about 13,200 lb, so the load is now 88 % of tipping, past what the chart allows. On a bigger swing it reaches about 49 ft, where the crane would tip with less than the load on the hook. Swing slowly, start and stop gently, and use tag lines.

The formula

On outriggers: chart ≤ 85 % of tipping (rubber often 75 %)
Tipping line: through the two load-side floats
Working area changes the chart: side, front, rear
Read at the loaded radius: deflection counts
Swing reach = line length × sin angle

On outriggers the chart is at most 85 % of the tipping load: 23,546 × 0.85 = 20,014 lb. Multiply by 0.85, don't divide. That 15 % covers deflection, swing, wind and ground; it isn't yours to use.

Worked exam questions

These questions are from the video. Try each one before you read the solution.

WORKED EXAMPLE 1

At a given radius, a crane on fully extended outriggers would tip with 23,546 lb on the hook. The chart rates loads on outriggers at no more than 85 % of tipping. What is the most the chart can show?

  • A. 27,700 lb
  • B. 23,546 lb
  • C. 20,014 lb
  • D. 17,660 lb

Answer: C. 20,014 lb

Multiply the tipping load by 0.85.

23,546 × 0.85 = 20,014 lb (charts round down: 20,000 lb)

20,014 lb. It's C. A divides by 0.85. B is the tipping load itself. D uses 75 %, the figure some charts use on rubber.

WORKED EXAMPLE 2

Radius before loading is 39 ft. With the load on, the boom deflects and the radius grows 1.4 ft. The chart lists 20,000 lb at 40 ft and 13,500 lb at 50 ft. Which rating do you use?

  • A. 20,000 lb
  • B. 16,750 lb
  • C. 13,500 lb
  • D. 23,546 lb

Answer: C. 13,500 lb

39 plus 1.4 is 40.4 ft. That's past 40, and you don't average between rows. You go to the next greater radius.

Loaded radius 39 + 1.4 = 40.4 ft > 40 → next greater radius on the chart, 50 ft: 13,500 lb

13,500. It's C. A uses the radius before loading. B averages the rows. D is a tipping load, not a rating.

Common mistakes

  • Reading the chart at the radius before loading
  • Using the over-side chart over the front
  • Treating 85 % as spare capacity
  • Forgetting a swinging load is a bigger radius
  • Overloading a shaded (structural) cell

Read the chart at the loaded radius and the next greater row, use the chart for the working area the boom is in, add swing reach to the radius, and never overload a shaded cell.
