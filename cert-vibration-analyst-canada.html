Vibration analyst certification in Canada: CAT I to IV

Vibration analysis is one of the best next steps for a millwright who likes finding problems before they break. Here is who certifies analysts in Canada, what each category asks for, and how it ties to your Red Seal exam.

Facts on this page were checked against each body's own website as of November 2026. Fees and rules change. Check with the body before you apply.

What it is and who it's for

A vibration analyst collects and reads vibration data from running machines. The goal is to find unbalance, misalignment, looseness and bearing wear early. Certification is built on the international standard ISO 18436-2, which sets four categories (CAT I to CAT IV). CAT I is for people who collect route data. CAT II and up are for people who analyse it and recommend repairs.

It suits millwrights in mills, mines, plants and utilities who already do predictive maintenance, or want to.

Required or voluntary?

Voluntary. We found no province that requires vibration analyst certification to do the work. Some employers ask for it in job postings or for predictive maintenance roles.

Exam format

None of the three bodies gave the number of questions or time limit on the public pages we opened. The Vibration Institute says sample questions are in its Certification Handbook. Check current details with the body before you book.

Renewal

  • Vibration Institute: valid five years. Renew by points (23 for CAT I up to 32 for CAT IV) or by re-examination (recertification page).
  • CMVA: five years from the exam date. Renew with work-activity points (20 for CAT 1, 25 for CAT 2, 30 for CAT 3-4) and a fee. The fee is waived if you were a member for the whole term (re-certification page).

How it connects to the Red Seal exam

In the Red Seal exam weightings for Industrial Mechanic (Millwright), vibration analysis and balancing sit in task F-25, "Performs maintenance and testing". Major work activity F is 13 % of the 135-question exam, and F-25 is 70 % of F. That works out to about 12 questions for all of F-25 (our estimate), and vibration is only part of that task. The bigger win is in task C-16, shaft alignment, and C-12, bearings: most vibration problems are an alignment or bearing problem underneath.

Practice questions

Written by CrewForge for practice. Not taken from any certification exam.

1. A motor runs at 1,780 rpm. What is its running speed (1×) in hertz?

About 29.7 Hz. Hertz is cycles per second. 1,780 rev per minute ÷ 60 s = 29.67 Hz.

2. A reading shows 0.20 in/s peak velocity from a clean sine wave. What is the RMS value, in imperial and metric?

0.141 in/s RMS, or 3.59 mm/s RMS. For a pure sine wave, RMS = 0.707 × peak. 0.707 × 0.20 = 0.141 in/s. In metric, 0.20 in/s × 25.4 = 5.08 mm/s peak, and 0.707 × 5.08 = 3.59 mm/s RMS. Always check which one your meter shows before you compare to a limit.

3. A fan has a high 1× reading in the radial direction. It drops a lot after you clean dirt off the blades. What was the likely cause?

Unbalance. Built-up dirt adds mass on one side of the wheel. That heavy spot pulls the rotor around once per turn, so it shows at 1×. Cleaning removed the mass.

4. A pinion with 22 teeth turns at 1,200 rpm. What is the gear mesh frequency?

440 Hz. Gear mesh frequency = number of teeth × shaft speed in Hz. 1,200 ÷ 60 = 20 Hz, and 22 × 20 = 440 Hz.

5. Vibration doubles after a coupling job. What should you check first?

Alignment and soft foot. A change right after work on the coupling points to the work. Check the alignment readings and soft foot before you look for a bearing fault.
