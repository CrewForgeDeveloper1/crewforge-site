How to read a crane load chart: radius, gross load and deflection

The chart says 20,000 lb and the load weighs 19,500 lb. That looks like 500 lb to spare, but it isn't. Millwrights don't run the crane, but they rig the load, give the operator the weight and often check the numbers. The Red Seal exam expects you to read a load chart. The chart on this page is an example made for training, not a real crane's chart. On the job, use the chart in the cab of the crane you're using.

Boom length across, radius down

A load chart is a grid. Boom lengths run across the top and the radius runs down the side. Where they meet is the capacity. On our example chart (training only, not a real crane), a 64 ft boom at a 30 ft radius is good for 24,800 lb.

The radius is the level distance from the centre of rotation to the centre of the hook, with the load hanging. It is not the boom length, and it is not measured from the outriggers or the bumper. Boom length and boom angle set it: R = L × cos θ. Lower the boom until the radius is 40 ft and the same boom drops to 20,000 lb. A lower boom means a longer radius, and a longer radius means less capacity.

Check the gross load, not the load

Chart numbers are gross capacity. Everything that hangs from the boom counts against them, not just the load.

  • Chart numbers are gross capacity
  • Hook block: 623 lb on this crane
  • Slings, shackles, spreader bar
  • Ball on the whipline (369 lb) and the auxiliary nose (105 lb)
  • Extension erected: 2,750 lb off the main boom chart

Build it up on the example crane: an 8,000 lb load, a 650 lb hook block, 150 lb of slings and shackles, a 350 lb ball on the whip line and 1,200 lb for the stowed jib (from the chart notes). The gross load is 10,350 lb. That is the number you check. Against 24,800 lb at a 30 ft radius, it is about 42 % of the chart. Makers don't all count things the same way, so read the notes for your crane.

The radius grows when the load lifts

A boom is a long beam, and it bends under load. Take a 16,000 lb load at a 38.5 ft radius on the example crane. Sitting on the ground, it reads the 40 ft row: 20,000 lb, about 92 %. Lift it and the tip moves out about a foot and a half. The radius is now just past 40 ft, so you read the 50 ft row: 13,500 lb. The lift is now at 136 % of the chart.

Measure the radius with the load hanging. When you plan, start with a higher boom angle to allow for the bend.

Between the listed values

Real booms and radii rarely land on a printed number. Chart notes cover this: when the boom length or the radius falls between listed values, use the smaller rating. On a normal chart that means the next longer boom and the next greater radius. A 57 ft boom at 33 ft reads the 64 ft column and the 35 ft row: 23,200 lb on the example chart. Don't work out your own numbers between the rows unless the chart says you can.

Shaded numbers and the 85 % rule

Some numbers on a chart are shaded or marked with a star. Those are structural ratings: the strength of the boom sets the limit, not tipping. On the example chart, 29,000 lb at 20 ft is shaded. Further out, at 50 ft, the 13,500 lb rating isn't shaded. The limit there is stability. On outriggers, those numbers are no more than 85 % of the load that would tip the crane. On tires or crawlers the figure is 75 %.

That 15 % gap is not yours to use. Wind, a soft spot under a float or a swinging load can take it all. In the shaded area it is worse: the boom runs out of strength before the crane feels light. A crane that feels steady is no proof the lift is safe.

Set-up changes the chart

The chart you read must match the set-up. On the example crane, a 64 ft boom at 30 ft over the side reads 24,800 lb with the outriggers fully extended. Pull the beams in and the tipping line moves in toward the crane: the retracted chart reads 7,400 lb. Swing over the front with the beams out, and this example crane reads 13,900 lb. Every crane is different. Read the chart for your outrigger position, your work area and your jib. Never lift with the beams part way out unless the chart covers that position.

The formula

Gross = load + hook block + rigging + ball and nose + erected extension
Chart value = gross capacity
Radius: level, centre of rotation to hook, load on
Not listed: next longer radius, lower column
Each outrigger setting has its own chart; the LMI must match it

Add everything that hangs from the boom to get the gross load. Find the radius with the load on (R = L × cos θ, plus the boom's bend), read the chart for your set-up, and divide the gross load by the chart value to get the percentage of the chart. Between listed values, use the smaller rating. The chart numbers used on this page come from an example chart made for training. It is not a real crane's chart.

Worked exam questions

These questions are from the video. Try each one before you read the solution.

WORKED EXAMPLE 1

A 12,500 lb load. Hook block 623 lb, slings and shackles 180 lb, a 369 lb ball on the whipline and a 105 lb auxiliary nose. No extension. What gross load do you check against the chart?

  • A. 12,500 lb
  • B. 13,303 lb
  • C. 13,777 lb
  • D. 16,527 lb

Answer: C. 13,777 lb

Add everything that hangs from the boom. 12,500, plus 623, plus 180, plus 369, plus 105. 13,777 lb.

12,500 + 623 + 180 + 369 + 105 = 13,777 lb

A is the load alone, the most common mistake. B forgets the ball and its nose. D adds an extension that isn't there.

WORKED EXAMPLE 2

60 ft boom, outriggers 100 %, loaded radius 43 ft. Load 11,000 lb, hook block 623 lb, slings 150 lb, whipline ball 369 lb and its nose 105 lb. Chart (60 ft boom): 14,450 lb at 40 ft, 11,650 lb at 45 ft. What percentage of the chart is the lift?

  • A. 94.4 %
  • B. 101.1 %
  • C. 105.1 %
  • D. 84.8 %

Answer: C. 105.1 %

First the gross load. 11,000, plus 623, 150, 369 and 105. 12,247. Next the row. 43 is past 40, so you read 45 ft. 11,650. 12,247 divided by 11,650. 105.1 %.

Gross = 11,000 + 623 + 150 + 369 + 105 = 12,247 lb; 43 ft → 45 ft row = 11,650 lb; 12,247 ÷ 11,650 = 105.1 % → over the chart

Over the chart. A is the load alone. D read the shorter radius. That 650 lb was never there.

Common mistakes

  • Load checked, not the gross load
  • Radius measured along the boom or from the outrigger
  • Reading the shorter radius or averaging rows
  • Wrong chart: outriggers or extension
  • Trusting an LMI set up for a different configuration

Check the gross load, measure the radius with the load hanging, and read the chart for the set-up you actually have.
