Machine moving: skates, air casters and toe jacks

Put a machine on three skates rated for more than its weight, and one can still collapse. To move a machine across a shop floor, millwrights lift one end with a toe jack, slide skates or air casters under it, and push or pull it into place. The Red Seal exam asks what each skate carries, what pressure an air caster needs, how much force it takes to move, and why loads tip. Rules differ by province, so check yours.

The centre of gravity decides the share

A 12,000 lb machine sits on four skates, two at the back and two at the front, 6 ft apart. With the centre of gravity in the middle, each skate carries a quarter, 3,000 lb. Move the centre of gravity to 4 ft ahead of the back skates and take moments about the back skates: the front pair carries 12,000 × 4 ÷ 6 = 8,000 lb, or 4,000 lb each, and the back skates carry 2,000 lb each.

Adding up the tags misses this. A 16,000 lb machine on three skates rated 6,000 lb each has 18,000 lb of skate, but the single swivel skate at the front carries the whole front share alone: half the machine, 8,000 lb, if the centre of gravity is in the middle, and more if the front is the heavy end. That skate collapses. Three skates are still worth using, because three points always sit flat, while four on an uneven floor can rock onto two. Put the pair under the heavy end and the single skate under the light end. With the centre of gravity 2 ft ahead of the pair on 6 ft spacing, all three skates carry 5,333 lb, under 6,000.

The force to move it, and tipping on a ramp

The skate makers give about 5 % of the load to keep machine skates rolling on concrete: 12,000 × 0.05 = 600 lb on a level floor, against nearly 2,000 lb on greased steel skids. A 2° ramp adds the weight times the sine of 2°, 419 lb, so about 1,019 lb to push it up. Going down at 2° the skates still hold it, but steeper than about 3° it would roll on its own, so the restraint line stays on every time.

A tall load tips when the plumb line from its centre of gravity passes the skates. On a slope it tips backward when the tangent of the angle is more than the distance of the centre of gravity ahead of the back skates over its height. A 6,000 lb cabinet with its centre of gravity 6 ft up and 1 ft ahead of the back skates tips at about 9.5°, so a 10° ramp takes it over. Keep the centre of gravity low and centred, spread the skates, and check before any ramp.

Air casters: pressure is load over area

An air caster is a flexible ring, a torus, under a flat plate. Shop air seals the ring to the floor, and when the pressure carries the load, air leaks out under the edge in a film 0.003 to 0.005 in thick and the load floats. Lift is pressure times area. A 21 in caster has an effective area of about 280 in². A centred 16,800 lb load puts 4,200 lb on each of four casters: 4,200 ÷ 280 = 15 psi. Standard casters are rated at 25 psi. Move the centre of gravity forward and the front casters need more pressure than the back, which is why each caster gets its own regulator. On 12 in casters (100 in²) the same load would need 42 psi, over the rating.

Air casters need sealed, smooth concrete; a floor within 1/4 in in any 10 ft circle is fine. At an open joint the air escapes, the film is gone and the caster grounds on its pads. Fill cracks and joints or lay a thin steel overlay before the move. On air it takes only about 5 lb per 1,000 lb to move, so a load drifts down even a 1 % grade: for 14,000 lb the maker says to plan a restraint for the full downhill pull, 140 lb.

Toe jacks: use the rating for the part you lift on

A toe jack's toe slides under a base with only about 1 in of clearance. Lifting one end, the far end stays on the floor, so the toe carries the weight times the distance from the centre of gravity to the far end, over the length. For a 12,000 lb machine 10 ft long with its centre of gravity 4 ft from the lifted end: 12,000 × 6 ÷ 10 = 7,200 lb.

Many toe jacks carry two ratings, such as 5 tons on the toe and 7 tons on the head, because the toe is out past the ram. Some makers rate the toe at full capacity. Either way, use the rating for the part you lift on; you can't borrow the head rating. Never lift on the tip of the toe: the load sits fully on it, back toward the jack body. Lift a little at a time and crib under the machine before the skates go in.

The formula

Front skates = W × CG ÷ spacing (÷ 2 for a pair)
Skates move at about 5 % of the load; air about 0.5 %
Tips when tan θ > CG ahead ÷ CG height
Air caster: pressure = load on the caster ÷ effective area; sealed floor, overlays
Toe jack: rating for the part you lift on; toe load = W × (L − CG) ÷ L

Take moments about the back skates: the front pair carries the weight times the centre of gravity distance over the spacing. 20,000 × 5 ÷ 8 = 12,500 lb for the pair, 6,250 lb on each front skate. Never just divide by the number of skates.

Worked exam questions

These questions are from the video. Try each one before you read the solution.

WORKED EXAMPLE 1

A 20,000 lb machine sits on four skates, two at the front and two at the back, 8 ft apart. Its centre of gravity is 5 ft ahead of the back skates. What does each front skate carry?

  • A. 5,000 lb
  • B. 6,250 lb
  • C. 3,750 lb
  • D. 12,500 lb

Answer: B. 6,250 lb

Moments about the back skates. The front pair carries 20,000 times 5, over 8. 12,500. Split between two skates.

Front pair = 20,000 × 5 ÷ 8 = 12,500 lb → 6,250 lb each (back skates 3,750 lb each)

6,250 lb each. It's B. A just divides by 4. C is a back skate. D is the whole front pair.

WORKED EXAMPLE 2

An air caster with an effective area of 140 in² carries 3,500 lb. What air pressure does it need to float?

  • A. 25 psi
  • B. 40 psi
  • C. 0.04 psi
  • D. 250 psi

Answer: A. 25 psi

Pressure is force over area. 3,500 over 140.

P = F ÷ A = 3,500 ÷ 140 = 25 psi

25 p s i. A. B divides the area by the load in thousands. C divides the wrong way.

Common mistakes

  • Dividing the weight equally between skates
  • Forgetting the single front skate of a 3-skate setup carries a whole share
  • Using the head rating while lifting on the toe
  • Ignoring CG height on a ramp
  • Floating air casters over open joints or slopes with no restraint

Work out each skate's share from the centre of gravity, give the single skate of a three-skate set a whole share, check the toe rating on a toe lift, and restrain loads on air casters.
