Spreader bars vs lifting beams: compression, buckling, bending and headroom

A spreader bar and a lifting beam both hang under the hook and hold the slings apart, but they work in opposite ways: a spreader bar is pushed end to end, and a lifting beam is bent. Rig a long, light spreader with flatter top slings to save headroom and it can buckle under the same load it carried safely at 60°. The ratings below are examples only: a real device is engineered and tagged, and you go by the tag.

No device: the slings squeeze the load

Hang a 10,000 lb load from a two-leg bridle with the picks 10 ft apart and the slings at 60° from horizontal. Each sling holds up half the load, 5,000 lb, but it pulls at an angle, so it also pulls the top of the load inward. That squeeze is C = (W ÷ 2) ÷ tan θ = 5,000 ÷ 1.732 = 2,887 lb on each side (the sling tension, (W ÷ 2) ÷ sin θ, is 5,774 lb). At 45° it climbs to 5,000 lb, enough to bow a long skid or crush a thin panel.

Spreader bar: compression, sling angle and buckling

A spreader bar takes the squeeze as compression (2,887 lb in the example above). The top slings run from the hook to the bar ends, and the drop slings hang straight down. Modulift wants the lower slings within 6° of vertical and says its bars are designed for axial compression, not bending: never hang a load from the tube.

Flatter top slings push harder. With 12,000 lb under the bar, half the load is 6,000 lb: at 60° the push is 3,464 lb, at 45° it is 6,000 lb, and at 30° it is 10,392 lb. That's why a spreader is rated at a sling angle. On the Modulift MOD 70 chart, a 30 ft bar is rated 51 t at 60° but only 29 t at 45°, and the angle must be 45° or more. Read your bar's own tag and chart, and stay at its angle or steeper.

A long, thin bar doesn't crush; it buckles. Euler's formula gives the buckling load of a slender bar: Pcr = π²EI ÷ L². A 20 ft NPS 3 Schedule 40 pipe buckles near 14,992 lb. Because the length is squared, the same pipe buckles near 26,652 lb at 15 ft but only 6,663 lb at 30 ft. ASME BTH-1, the US design standard for below-the-hook devices, uses a design factor of 3 for most of them (Category B).

Lifting beam: bending and stress

A lifting beam hangs from one bail at its centre, with drop slings at the ends. There are no angled top slings, so the sling angle doesn't change its rating. The beam bends, worst at the centre: M = W × s ÷ 4. For 10,000 lb on a 10 ft beam, M = 25,000 lb·ft, or 300,000 lb·in. Bending stress is σ = M ÷ S; a W8×18 (S = 15.2 in³) sees about 19,737 psi. Rated at a third of a 50,000 psi yield, as in our example, that beam is over; a W10×22 (S = 23.2 in³) drops the stress to about 12,931 psi. Change feet to inches before you divide by S.

Headroom, device weight and the rules

Headroom is the height from the hook to the top of the load. For the same 10,000 lb, 10 ft, 60° pick with 4 ft drops: no device needs 8.66 ft, a spreader bar needs 5 × 1.732 + 4 = 12.66 ft, and a lifting beam needs about 5.85 ft in our example. When the hook can't go higher, a beam is often the answer; a spreader is usually lighter for the same capacity.

The device is part of the load. A 10 ft W10×22 beam weighs 220 lb, so a 9,800 lb machine puts 10,020 lb on the hook before you add the slings and shackles. In BC (OHS Regulation s. 15.57 to 15.60) a below-the-hook device must meet ASME B30.20, have its WLL set by the maker or a professional engineer, and be marked with the maker, serial number, WLL and its weight if over 45 kg (100 lb). Alberta's OHS Code Part 21 is similar, and Ontario's Reg. 851 also requires lifting devices to be marked and inspected. Check your province.

The formula

No device: the slings squeeze the load, C = (W ÷ 2) ÷ tan θ
Spreader bar: compression; flatter slings → more push → lower rated load
Slender bars buckle: Pcr = π²EI ÷ L²; design factor 3 (ASME BTH-1, Category B)
Lifting beam: bending, M = W × s ÷ 4, σ = M ÷ S; least headroom
Device weight is part of the load; rated, marked, engineered, inspected

Half the load divided by the tangent of the top-sling angle gives the push: the squeeze on the load with no device, or the compression in a spreader bar. For 10,000 lb at 60°: 5,000 ÷ 1.732 = 2,887 lb. Flatter slings make tan θ smaller and the push bigger.

Worked exam questions

These questions are from the video. Try each one before you read the solution.

WORKED EXAMPLE 1

A 16,000 lb load hangs from a two-leg bridle with no spreader. The slings are at 60° from horizontal. What is the inward squeeze at each pick point?

  • A. 8,000 lb
  • B. 9,238 lb
  • C. 4,619 lb
  • D. 13,856 lb

Answer: C. 4,619 lb

Each leg holds up half, 8,000 lb. Divide by the tangent of 60, 1.732.

Each leg holds W ÷ 2 = 8,000 lb up; squeeze = 8,000 ÷ tan 60° = 8,000 ÷ 1.732 = 4,619 lb

4,619 lb. It's C. A is just half the load. B is the sling tension, not the squeeze. D multiplies by the tangent instead of dividing.

WORKED EXAMPLE 2

A spreader bar is 12 ft between its sling points. The top slings are at 60° from horizontal and the drop slings are 5 ft long. About how much headroom is needed from the hook to the top of the load?

  • A. 10.39 ft
  • B. 15.39 ft
  • C. 25.78 ft
  • D. 8.46 ft

Answer: B. 15.39 ft

The top slings rise over half the span, 6 ft. 6 times the tangent of 60 is 10.39 ft. Add the 5 ft drops.

Top-sling height = (12 ÷ 2) × tan 60° = 6 × 1.732 = 10.39 ft; + 5 ft drop slings = 15.39 ft

15.39 ft. It's B. A forgets the drop slings. C uses the whole span instead of half. D divides by the tangent instead of multiplying.

Common mistakes

  • Dividing by sin θ (tension) when the question asks for compression (÷ tan θ)
  • Flattening the top slings to save headroom
  • Forgetting the device's weight on the hook
  • Hanging a load from the middle of a spreader bar
  • Leaving feet in M when dividing by S in in³

Divide by tan θ for compression (sin θ gives the sling tension), never flatten the top slings to save headroom, add the device's weight, and change lb·ft to lb·in before dividing by S.
