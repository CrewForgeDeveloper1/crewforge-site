Canadian hoisting and rigging rules: OHS regulations, CSA and ASME standards, inspections

A wire rope sling fails inspection, someone pulls it from the scrap bin, and it parts at 80 % of its rating. Every province writes its own hoisting and rigging rules, and the Red Seal exam expects you to know how they fit together. This page uses three provinces as examples, British Columbia, Alberta and Ontario; yours will be similar, but check its own rules. It's an overview for the exam, not legal advice.

Four layers of rules

On top is the law: your province's occupational health and safety act and the regulation under it. That's what an inspector enforces. Next come the standards the regulation adopts by name: mostly CSA standards for cranes, and American ASME standards for slings and below-the-hook devices. A standard becomes law where the regulation names it; BC's s. 15.30, for example, adopts the ASME sling standard. Then the manufacturer's data: the tag, the manual and the load chart, which most regulations tell you to follow. Last, your site's own procedures and the lift plan. They can be stricter than the law, never looser.

Where the rules are

In British Columbia it's the OHS Regulation: Part 14 for cranes and hoists, Part 15 for rigging. In Alberta it's the OHS Code: Part 6 for cranes, hoists and lifting devices, Part 21 for rigging. Ontario splits it by workplace: Regulation 851, for industrial establishments like mills and plants, covers lifting devices in s. 51, and O. Reg. 213/91 covers construction projects, with cranes and rigging from s. 150 on. Federally regulated employers, like ports, railways and grain terminals, follow the Canada Occupational Health and Safety Regulations, Part XIV, materials handling.

For overhead cranes, gantry cranes and hoists the Canadian standard is CSA B167, adopted in BC (s. 14.2) and Alberta (s. 94). Mobile cranes follow CSA Z150 and tower cranes CSA Z248; Ontario's construction regulation uses them to set crane ratings. There's no CSA sling standard: BC (s. 15.30) and Alberta (s. 297) adopt ASME B30.9 for slings and ASME B30.20 for below-the-hook devices like spreader beams and lifting clamps. A regulation names a particular edition, often an older one, and that's the edition the law requires. These standards are licensed, so they're cited here by number only.

Tags, and who can do the work

In BC an alloy chain sling's tag shows the size, grade, working load limit, length, number of legs and maker (s. 15.46). A web sling's shows the maker, a code or stock number, the rated load for each hitch and the material (s. 15.52). Alberta (s. 293) wants the load rating legibly marked, and Ontario's construction regulation wants slings labelled with their rating. No tag, no lift: without it you can't prove the rating, so the sling comes out of service until the maker or a qualified person re-rates and re-tags it.

BC uses the word qualified, and s. 15.2 says rigging is done or directly supervised by qualified workers. Ontario uses competent person, and Alberta uses competent; the definitions differ, so learn your province's. Crane operators need more, a certificate: BC requires one for mobile cranes, tower cranes and boom trucks, and Ontario requires a hoisting engineer certificate of qualification for most mobile and tower cranes on construction projects.

Inspections, removal and records

In BC, slings get a visual check before each shift (s. 15.31) and chain slings a recorded thorough inspection at least once a year (s. 15.50). Alberta inspects rigging before each period of continuous use in a shift, and lifting devices keep a log book. In Ontario plants, a competent person examines lifting devices before first use, as often as needed, never less often than the maker says and at least once a year, with a signed record (Reg. 851, s. 51). On Ontario construction, crane cables and alloy chain are checked at least weekly while in use, and logged. If it isn't written down, the inspector can't tell it was done.

The regulations also set removal criteria. For running rope on a crane, BC, Alberta and Ontario's construction rule all use six broken wires in one lay, or three in one strand in one lay; BC and Alberta also take 3/4 in rope out when it's worn more than 3/64 in. The usual limit for a wire rope sling is ten broken wires in one lay. BC bans proof coil and transport chain for hoisting (s. 15.47), all three provinces forbid welding or annealing alloy chain, and BC takes a hook out at 15 % throat opening or 10° of twist. Ontario construction slings must support at least five times the maximum load (s. 172), ten times for rigging that supports people. Alberta requires a lift calculation for any lift over 75 % of a crane's rated capacity (s. 68.1). When a rule and the maker differ, the stricter one wins, and defective rigging comes out of service right away. A rejected sling stays rejected.

The formula

Law → adopted standards → maker's data → site rules
BC Part 14/15 · Alberta Part 6/21 · Ontario 851 and 213/91
CSA B167, Z150, Z248 · ASME B30.9, B30.20
Tag, inspect, record
Rejected means rejected

Work down the layers: the provincial OHS law, then the CSA or ASME standard it adopts by name, then the maker's tag, manual and chart, then your site's procedures and lift plan. A lower layer can be stricter than the one above it, never looser. And when a rule and the maker differ, the stricter one wins.

Worked exam questions

These questions are from the video. Try each one before you read the solution.

WORKED EXAMPLE 1

On a provincially regulated work site in Canada, what sets the legal requirements for rigging?

  • A. ASME B30.9 on its own
  • B. The provincial OHS act and regulation, and the standards it adopts
  • C. The sling maker's catalogue only
  • D. The Red Seal Occupational Standard

Answer: B. The provincial OHS act and regulation, and the standards it adopts

The provincial OHS act and regulation, plus the standards it adopts. A standard becomes law when the regulation names it. British Columbia's section 15.30, for example, adopts the ASME sling standard.

OHS law governs; a standard applies where adopted (e.g. BC s.15.30)

A is only law where it's adopted. C is part of the picture, not all of it. And D describes the trade for training. It isn't a safety law.

WORKED EXAMPLE 2

On an Ontario construction project, slings must support at least 5 times the maximum load (O. Reg. 213/91 s.172). A sling breaks at 10,000 kg. What is the most it may carry?

  • A. 5,000 kg
  • B. 2,500 kg
  • C. 2,000 kg
  • D. 1,000 kg

Answer: C. 2,000 kg

10,000 divided by 5 is 2,000 kg.

10,000 kg ÷ 5 = 2,000 kg (s.172(1)(d))

B used 4 : 1. D used 10 : 1, the factor for rigging that supports people. A is half the breaking strength, nowhere near safe.

Common mistakes

  • Treating ASME as law everywhere
  • Using rigging that failed inspection
  • No tag, but lifting anyway
  • Mixing up qualified, competent and certified
  • No record of the inspection

ASME is law only where your regulation adopts it, a rejected sling stays rejected, no tag means no lift, and an inspection that isn't recorded didn't happen as far as an inspector can tell.
