Block and tackle: mechanical advantage

A wire rope block and tackle hung from a beam clamp, lifting a test weight. Reeve 1 to 8 parts of line, pick the sheave friction, then pull the hand line. Watch the pull, how far the lead line travels, where the effort goes, and the load on the anchor.

Open the simulator

Open full screen · Exam preparation only: always check the block and anchor ratings, the manufacturer's data and your lift plan.

Exam tip: count only the parts of line holding the moving block. The lead line counts only when it leaves the moving block. And the anchor carries the load plus your pull.

What this simulator works out

The pull needed on the lead line of a block and tackle with 1 to 8 parts of line, how far the lead line travels for each foot or metre of lift, the work lost to sheave friction, and the load on the top block and the anchor. A second simulator covers chain falls.

The formula in words

The ideal mechanical advantage equals the number of parts of line holding the moving block, so the ideal pull is the load divided by the parts. A common allowance for friction (the 10 % rule) adds 10 % of the load for every sheave the rope runs over. The lead line travels the number of parts times the lift.

Worked example

  • Imperial: 4,000 lb on 4 parts, lead line off the fixed block (4 sheaves): ideal pull 4,000 ÷ 4 = 1,000 lb. With the 10 % rule, 4,000 × 1.4 ÷ 4 = 1,400 lb. Lifting 2 ft takes 4 × 2 = 8 ft of lead line. The anchor carries 4,000 + 1,400 = 5,400 lb.
  • Metric: 2,000 kg on the same 4-part reeving: ideal pull 500 kg. With the 10 % rule, 2,000 × 1.4 ÷ 4 = 700 kg, about 6.86 kN. Lifting 1 m takes 4 m of lead line.
