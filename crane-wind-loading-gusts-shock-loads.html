Wind and dynamic loading: wind force, gusts, wind area and shock loads

In a steady 30 mph wind a crane can hold a load that the same wind, arriving as a sudden gust, tips over. A load chart assumes a load hanging straight down, still air and smooth lifting. On a real job you get wind, gusts and the occasional jerk on the line. Millwrights rig wide panels, fan housings and ductwork, the loads wind loves most, and the Red Seal exam asks about wind limits and shock loading. The crane numbers here come from one maker's product guide, for training only.

Wind force goes with the speed squared

Wind pressure in pounds per square foot is 0.00256 times the wind speed in mph, squared. At 20 mph that's about 1 psf. Force is the pressure times the area facing the wind times a shape factor, about 1.2 for a box. A fan housing 20 ft wide and 10 ft high has a 200 ft² face, so at 20 mph the wind pushes on it with about 246 lb. At 30 mph the pressure is 2.304 psf and the force about 553 lb. Double the wind and the force goes up four times, not two. In metric, pressure in pascals is 0.613 times the speed in m/s, squared.

That force pushes the load sideways while gravity pulls it down, and the hook line leans until it lines up with the two: the force triangle. At 20 mph the angle is under one degree, but on a 70 ft line the load moves out about a foot. At 40 mph it leans four times as far.

A gust swings it twice as far

A load hit suddenly doesn't stop at its new lean; it swings past it, like a pendulum let go from the wrong spot. It swings out to about twice the jump. A panel crate of 19,823 lb gross with a 500 ft² face, at 91 % of the chart, leans about half a foot in a 10 mph breeze. Raise the wind slowly to a steady 30 mph and it leans about 3.5 ft, 85 % of the tipping load, and the crane holds. Hit it with the same 30 mph all at once and it swings past 37 ft of radius, where the tipping load is less than the load, and the crane goes. That's why maker charts give wind limits as three-second gusts measured at the boom tip, not as an average. Wind at the tip is stronger than at your hard hat.

Big-area loads get a lower wind limit

Grove's operator manual allows full rated capacity up to 30 mph, reduced capacity from 30 to 45 mph, and no lifting above 45. Those limits assume no more than 0.0059 ft² of wind area per pound of load; Liebherr puts it as 1.2 m² per tonne, the same thing. Wind area is the face area times the shape factor, and Grove says to use 2.4 if you don't know it. A light load with a big face is worse: the allowed wind drops by the square root of the ratio.

The fan housing has 200 × 1.2 = 240 ft² of wind area. At 15,823 lb the chart assumes no more than 93 ft², so this load has two and a half times that. Allowed wind: 30 × the square root of 93 ÷ 240, about 18.7 mph at the boom tip. In metric, a 20 t load with 48 m² of wind area has 2.4 m² per tonne, so a 9 m/s limit becomes 9 × the square root of (1.2 × 20 ÷ 48), about 6.4 m/s. In British Columbia, wind limits are written into every critical and tandem lift plan.

Dynamic loads: more than the weight

Under load the hoist line and the boom stretch a little, like a spring. Pick a load with slack in the line at full speed and the line snaps tight: a snatch. The peak is the weight times a dynamic factor, 1 plus the speed over the square root of g times the stretch. With 14,800 lb snatched at 1.5 ft/s and about 1 ft of stretch, the factor is about 1.26 and the peak about 18,600 lb: 93 % of a 20,000 lb chart from a lift that started at 74 %.

A load let go all at once onto a tight line, with no drop at all, pulls twice its weight. A load that drops onto the line, even a few inches, pulls more than twice. Take up slack slowly, lift smoothly, and never let a load hang up and fall. The 15 % between the chart and tipping doesn't cover all of this.

The formula

q = 0.00256 V² psf (0.613 v² Pa); F = q × A × Cw
Force triangle: tan φ = F ÷ W; reach = Lr sin φ
A sudden gust swings it about twice as far
Big area per tonne → lower allowed wind
Dynamic factor: suddenly applied 2 ×; a drop, more

Wind pressure is 0.00256 × V² psf with V in mph; force is that pressure × the face area × the shape factor. At 30 mph on 200 ft² with Cw 1.2: 0.00256 × 900 = 2.304 psf, × 200 × 1.2 = 553 lb. Square the speed first.

Worked exam questions

These questions are from the video. Try each one before you read the solution.

WORKED EXAMPLE 1

A panel has 200 ft² facing the wind and a shape factor of 1.2. Using q = 0.00256 × V² (psf), what is the wind force at 30 mph?

  • A. 15 lb
  • B. 184 lb
  • C. 553 lb
  • D. 2,304 lb

Answer: C. 553 lb

Pressure first. 0.00256 times 900, 2.304 lb/ft². Times 200, times 1.2.

q = 0.00256 × 30² = 2.304 psf; F = 2.304 × 200 × 1.2 = 553 lb

About 553 lb. It's C. A forgets to square the speed. B forgets the area is 200. D forgets to divide by 1,000.

WORKED EXAMPLE 2

A 10,000 lb load is released all at once onto a sling that is tight but carrying nothing, with no drop. About what peak force does the sling see?

  • A. 10,000 lb
  • B. 15,000 lb
  • C. 20,000 lb
  • D. 100,000 lb

Answer: C. 20,000 lb

Strength of materials has a classic answer. A load applied suddenly stretches the sling to twice its static stretch, so the peak force is twice the weight.

A suddenly applied load (no drop, no speed) gives twice the static force: 2 × 10,000 = 20,000 lb

20,000 lb. It's C. A is a load lowered gently onto it. With any drop at all, it's more than twice.

Common mistakes

  • Wind force ∝ speed, not speed²
  • Using the average wind, not the 3-second gust
  • Using the chart limit for a big-area, light load
  • Snatching slack or letting a load hang up and drop
  • Thinking the 15 % margin covers all of it

Square the wind speed, use the three-second gust at the boom tip, cut the wind limit for a light load with a big face, and never snatch slack or let a load drop onto the line.
