Block and tackle mechanical advantage: parts of line, friction and chain falls

How hard do you have to pull to lift a 4,000 lb gearbox with a block and tackle? It depends on how many parts of line hold the moving block, and on friction in the sheaves. Here is how to work it out, and how the Red Seal millwright exam tests it.

One sheave only changes direction

Hang one sheave from a beam, run a rope over it, and put the load on one end. Pull down and the load goes up, but a 1,000 lb load still takes a 1,000 lb pull, and the rope moves as far as the load. A single fixed sheave doesn't multiply anything. It only lets you pull down instead of lifting up. That is a mechanical advantage of 1.

Count the parts of line holding the moving block

Hang a second sheave on the load. That is the moving block, because it travels with the load; the one on the beam is the fixed block. Now two parts of line hold the moving block, each carries half the load, and a 1,000 lb load takes 500 lb of pull. To raise the load 1 ft, both parts must shorten by 1 ft, so you pull 2 ft of rope.

With two sheaves in each block you have a four-part tackle. Four parts hold the moving block, so a 4,000 lb load puts 1,000 lb in each part and the pull is 1,000 lb. The lead line that leaves the fixed block and runs down to your hands doesn't hold the moving block, so it doesn't count.

Reeving to advantage

The lead line counts only when it leaves the moving block. Take two single-sheave blocks. Reeved so the lead line leaves the fixed block, two parts hold the moving block and 900 lb takes 450 lb. Reeve the same blocks the other way, so the lead line leaves the moving block and you pull up: the dead end, the middle part and the lead line all hold the moving block. The advantage is 3 and the pull drops to 300 lb. Same blocks, one more part.

You trade distance for force

Lift a 4,000 lb load 2 ft with the four-part tackle and the lead line runs 8 ft, four times as far. That ratio of distances is the velocity ratio, and the reeving alone sets it. The work matches: 1,000 lb × 8 ft is 8,000 ft·lb in, and 4,000 lb × 2 ft is 8,000 ft·lb out. A tackle never gives you extra work.

Friction in the sheaves

Real sheaves turn on pins or bearings, and each one takes some of your pull. A common rule in rigging texts adds 10 % of the load for every sheave the rope runs over, then divides by the parts of line. With the lead line off the fixed block there is one sheave per part; off the moving block, one less. Maker's tables are kinder: Crosby's figures work out to about 4 % per bronze-bushed sheave and about 2 % per roller-bearing sheave. On the exam, use the method the question gives you.

On the four-part tackle with the 10 % rule, four sheaves add 40 %: 4,000 × 1.4 ÷ 4 = 1,400 lb. The four parts now pull 5,600 lb in total; 4,000 lb of it lifts the load and 1,600 lb is friction. The lead line still runs 8 ft for a 2 ft lift, so you put in 11,200 ft·lb to get 8,000 ft·lb out. With roller-bearing sheaves from the Crosby table, the same lift takes 1,050 lb; with bronze bushings, about 1,114 lb.

Velocity ratio, actual mechanical advantage and efficiency

  • Velocity ratio: lead travel ÷ load rise (set by the reeving)
  • Actual MA: W ÷ P (what you really get)
  • Efficiency = actual MA ÷ velocity ratio
  • With friction, actual MA is always less than the velocity ratio

The velocity ratio is a distance ratio that friction can't change. The actual mechanical advantage is a force ratio, the load divided by the pull you really need. With friction, the actual mechanical advantage is always less than the velocity ratio.

Chain falls

A geared hand chain hoist packs the same idea into a gearbox. One maker lists its 2 ton hoist at a 72 lb pull on the hand chain to lift the rated 4,000 lb, with an overhaul ratio of 70: 70 ft of hand chain for every foot of lift. That ratio is the velocity ratio. 70 × 72 is 5,040 against a 4,000 lb load, so the efficiency is about 79 %. If a chain fall ever needs much more pull than the maker lists, stop: the load may be heavier than you think, or something may be caught.

The older differential chain block has two pocket wheels of different sizes on one axle and an endless chain. With 10 in and 9 in wheels, the velocity ratio is 2D ÷ (D − d) = 2 × 10 ÷ 1 = 20. At 35 % efficiency, 1,000 lb takes about 143 lb of pull. Because it is under 50 % efficient, the load holds when you let go of the chain.

From the field

When you pull down on the lead line, the fixed block and its anchor carry the load plus your pull, so check them for that total. The load on a block also changes with the angle between the lines; use the maker's chart. More parts of line means a smaller pull but a slower hook. Blocks and hooks are built to break at about four times their rating, and that margin is not spare capacity: a top block rated 16,000 lb with 60,000 lb on the hook and a 15,000 lb pull carries 75,000 lb, almost five times its rating.

The formula

P = W ÷ n
n = parts of line holding the moving block
Ideal MA = n
Lead line travel = n × load rise
The lead line counts only if it leaves the moving block

With no friction, the pull on the lead line equals the load divided by the number of parts of line holding the moving block. The lead line travels that number of times the load's rise. With the 10 % rule, multiply the load by (1 + 0.10 × the number of sheaves) before you divide by the parts.

Worked exam questions

These questions are from the video. Try each one before you read the solution.

WORKED EXAMPLE 1

A tackle has a two-sheave upper block and a one-sheave lower block. The lead line leaves the upper block. What is the ideal mechanical advantage?

  • A. 2
  • B. 3
  • C. 4
  • D. 6

Answer: B. 3

Picture the reeving. The rope is dead ended on the lower block. It runs up over the first upper sheave, down around the lower sheave, up over the second upper sheave, and down to your hands. Three parts hold the lower block. The lead line leaves the fixed block, so it doesn't count.

Dead end on the lower block + 2 parts = 3 parts hold the moving block; lead line not counted

If you said four, you counted the lead line.

WORKED EXAMPLE 2

An 8,000 lb load is lifted with a 4-part tackle, lead line off the fixed block (4 sheaves). Allowing 10 % of the load per sheave for friction, what pull is needed?

  • A. 2,000 lb
  • B. 2,200 lb
  • C. 2,800 lb
  • D. 11,200 lb

Answer: C. 2,800 lb

Four sheaves at 10 % is 40 %. 8,000 times 1.4 is 11,200. Divide by 4 parts. 2,800 lb.

P = 8,000 × (1 + 0.10 × 4) ÷ 4 = 11,200 ÷ 4 = 2,800 lb

A ignores friction. B adds only one ten percent. And D forgot to divide by the parts.

Common mistakes

  • Counting the lead line when it leaves the fixed block
  • Missing it when it leaves the moving block
  • Ignoring friction, or adding 10 % only once
  • Mixing up velocity ratio and actual MA
  • Dividing the lift instead of multiplying the travel

Count the parts at the moving block, every time, and check which block the lead line leaves.
