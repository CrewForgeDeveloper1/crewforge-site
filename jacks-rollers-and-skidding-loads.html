Jacks, rollers and skidding loads: load share, F = P × A and slopes

A jack can be rated for the load and still drop it. The rating only counts with the jack plumb, on a firm base, under a proper jacking point, and with cribbing following the load. The Red Seal exam also expects you to work out how much a jack really carries, read a hydraulic jack with F = P × A, and find the pull needed to move a machine on skids, rollers or skates.

How much does one jack carry?

When you jack one end of a machine, the far end stays down and acts as a pivot, so the jack doesn't carry the whole weight. Take moments about the far end: the jack load is the weight times the distance from the pivot to the centre of gravity, divided by the distance from the pivot to the jack.

A 12,000 lb machine, 10 ft long, with its centre of gravity 4 ft from the jacked end: the weight acts 6 ft from the pivot and the jack 10 ft from it. 12,000 × 6 ÷ 10 = 7,200 lb on the jack, and the far end carries 4,800 lb. Move the centre of gravity to 2 ft from the jacked end and the jack's share climbs to 9,600 lb. The heavy end is the hard end. Two jacks side by side at that end share the load.

Hydraulic jacks: F = P × A

Every square inch of a hydraulic jack's ram gets the same oil pressure. Force equals pressure times ram area. A 10-ton jack with a 2.24 in² ram carrying 7,200 lb reads 7,200 ÷ 2.24 = 3,214 psi. At its 10,000 psi maximum it pushes 22,400 lb, but you work to the 10-ton rating. A 50-ton jack has an 11.04 in² ram, so the same load reads only 652 psi.

Bottle jacks need clearance under a jacking point. Toe jacks lift on a low toe, and the toe can be rated lower than the head, so read the tag. Screw jacks hold by their thread and won't bleed down.

Crib as you go

As soon as a load is raised, it gets cribbed. Raise a little, build the cribbing up under the load, raise again, crib again. The gap between the load and the cribbing is how far the load falls if the jack fails. A jack set out of plumb pushes sideways as well as up and can kick out; with cribbing right up under the load, it drops a fraction of an inch instead of all the way. Never put any part of your body under a load on jacks.

  • Bottle jack: hydraulic, needs clearance under a jacking point
  • Toe jack: lifts on a low toe; toe and head ratings can differ
  • Screw jack: holds by its thread, slow and steady
  • Rated load marked on every jack; never exceed it
  • Firm, blocked base · plumb · crib the load at once

Skidding, rollers and skates

On the level, the pull is the coefficient of friction (or rolling resistance) times the weight. For 8,000 lb: dry steel skids, about 0.42, need 3,360 lb; greased steel, about 0.16, 1,280 lb; steel rollers, about 0.05, 400 lb; skates with synthetic wheels, about 0.02, only 160 lb. That's 21 times less pull from dry steel to skates.

Rollers travel at half the load's speed, so they come out the back and are carried round to the front. Set and steer them with a bar, never your hands, and turn by angling the front rollers a little at a time. Three skates set around the centre of gravity all stay loaded on an uneven floor.

Slopes and control lines

On a slope the weight splits: W cos θ presses on the surface and W sin θ pulls the load downhill. Uphill you pull against both. For 10,000 lb on rollers up 5°: 0.05 × 9,962 + 872 = about 1,370 lb, almost three times the 500 lb on the flat. Rollers hold a load in place only below about 2.9°. Let the same load down 5° and the control line must hold 872 − 498 = about 374 lb. With no control line, it runs away.

The formula

Jack one end: R = W × (L − CG) ÷ (L − jack point)
Hydraulic jack: F = P × A
Crib as you go · never under a load on jacks
Level: pull = μ × W (skates 0.02 · rollers 0.05 · greased 0.16 · dry steel 0.42)
Slope: add W sin θ uphill; rig a control line downhill

Take moments about the end that stays down: the jack carries the weight times the centre of gravity's distance from that end, divided by the jack's distance from it. Then share it between the jacks, and divide by the ram area for the gauge pressure.

Worked exam questions

These questions are from the video. Try each one before you read the solution.

WORKED EXAMPLE 1

A pump base weighs 9,000 lb and is 8 ft long. Its centre of gravity is 3 ft from the end you're jacking; the other end stays down. What does the jack carry?

  • A. 3,375 lb
  • B. 4,500 lb
  • C. 5,625 lb
  • D. 9,000 lb

Answer: C. 5,625 lb

Take moments about the far end. The weight is 5 ft from it, the jack is 8. 9,000 times 5, divided by 8.

Moments about the far end: 9,000 × (8 − 3) ÷ 8 = 9,000 × 5 ÷ 8 = 5,625 lb

5,625 lb. It's C. A uses the wrong arm, 3 ft instead of 5. B is half the weight. D is the whole weight.

WORKED EXAMPLE 2

A 10,000 lb load on steel rollers (rolling resistance 0.05) is pulled up a 5° slope. About what pull is needed?

  • A. 500 lb
  • B. 872 lb
  • C. 1,370 lb
  • D. 2,740 lb

Answer: C. 1,370 lb

Uphill, add the two. 0.05 times the weight times the cosine of 5° is about 498. The weight times the sine of 5° is 872.

Uphill: μ W cos θ + W sin θ = 0.05 × 9,962 + 872 = 498 + 872 ≈ 1,370 lb

About 1,370 lb. C. A forgets the slope. B forgets the rollers. D just doubles the answer.

WORKED EXAMPLE 3

A 16,000 lb gearbox is 10 ft long, centre of gravity 4 ft from the end you're jacking. Two 5-ton bottle jacks sit side by side at that end, each with a 0.99 in² ram. What does each jack carry, and what does each gauge read?

  • A. 8,000 lb, about 8,080 psi
  • B. 4,800 lb, about 4,850 psi
  • C. 9,600 lb, about 9,700 psi
  • D. 3,200 lb, about 3,230 psi

Answer: B. 4,800 lb, about 4,850 psi

Moments about the far end first. 16,000 times 6, divided by 10, is 9,600 lb at the jacked end. Two jacks share it, so 4,800 each. That's 48 % of a five ton rating. Then pressure is force over area. 4,800, divided by 0.99.

Jacked end: 16,000 × 6 ÷ 10 = 9,600 lb; two jacks: 4,800 lb each (48 % of 10,000 lb); gauge 4,800 ÷ 0.99 ≈ 4,848 psi

About 4,850 p s i. It's B. A gives each jack half the whole weight. C forgets there are two jacks. D uses the wrong arm.

Common mistakes

  • Giving the jack the whole weight when one end stays down
  • Using the wrong lever arm: measure from the pivot
  • P ÷ A instead of P × A
  • Forgetting W sin θ on a slope
  • No control line going downhill
  • Trusting a jack instead of cribbing

Measure lever arms from the pivot, multiply pressure by area, add W sin θ on a slope, and crib the load every time.
