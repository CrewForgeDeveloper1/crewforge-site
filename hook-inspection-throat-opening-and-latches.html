Hooks: tip loading, latches and throat opening inspection

A hook rated three tonnes can open under far less than three tonnes if the sling rides out on the tip. Every lift hangs on the hook, so millwrights need to know where the load goes, how many legs can share a hook, what the latch does, and when a hook comes out of service. The Red Seal exam asks all four. Rules differ by province, so check yours.

Seat the load in the bowl

The bowl, also called the saddle, is the curved bottom of the hook. A load seated there hangs on the line of the shank: straight down from where the hook hangs. There is no lever arm, and the hook carries its full working load limit.

When the sling eye rides up toward the tip, the load line moves away from the shank. The load now works on a lever arm, and the load times that arm bends the hook open on top of the straight pull. In the simulator's example (built on one maker's average for foundry hooks, about 30 % at the tip), a 3,000 kg hook is good for about 989 kg half way out and 900 kg at the tip. For hoist hooks the makers' rule is simpler: never tip load. Kito Crosby also warns against side loading (a pull out of the plane of the hook) and back loading (load on the back of the hook or the latch). All three damage the hook and reduce its capacity.

Two legs in one hook

Two sling legs can share the bowl, but the angle between them, the included angle, must be no more than 90°. That is no more than 45° from the hook's centreline on each side. Two legs each 50° from the centreline make 100° included: too wide. Put the legs on a master link or collector ring and hang the link in the bowl. Do the same if you have more than two legs.

The latch keeps the sling in

The spring latch closes the throat so a slack or swinging sling can't jump out. It never carries load. If anything pushes on the latch, the sling is out of the bowl. British Columbia's OHS Regulation (s. 15.10) requires a safety latch or another way to keep slings in the hook, with some exemptions. Alberta's OHS Code (s. 303) asks for a latch, mousing or a shackle where a dislodged load could hurt someone. Ontario's construction regulation also calls for a safety catch. A latch won't work on a hook with a bent or worn tip, and a bent tip means a damaged hook.

Inspect: throat, twist, wear, cracks

An overloaded hook opens before it breaks, so the throat opening is the record of its past. Measure it at the narrowest point and compare with the size when new. Throat change = (measured − new) ÷ new. A Crosby 3 t hook with a 34.5 mm throat that now measures 40.0 mm has opened 5.5 ÷ 34.5 = 15.9 %. WorkSafeBC (s. 15.29) removes a hook over 15 %. At 39.0 mm it has opened 13.0 %, under 15 %, but Kito Crosby says never use a hook whose throat opening has increased at all. When the maker's limit is stricter, it governs. Many hooks carry two deformation marks, one under the shank and one on the tip; if their spacing has changed, the hook has been overloaded or abused.

Also remove a hook that is twisted more than 10° from its original plane, has lost 10 % or more of its cross-section, has any crack, or is worn past the maker's limits (some of Crosby's wear zones allow only 5 %). Slings and their attachments get a visual check before use on each shift in British Columbia (s. 15.31), and before each period of continuous use in Alberta (s. 294), on top of periodic recorded inspections.

The formula

Throat change = (measured − new) ÷ new; remove over 15 % (BC), or any increase if the maker says so
Load in the bowl, on the line of the shank: no tip, side or back loading
Two legs in one hook: 90° included max (45° each side); beyond that, a master link
Latch: keeps the sling in, never carries load; a bent tip stops it working
Remove for twist over 10°, section loss 10 % or more, any crack; check before each shift

Throat change = (measured − new) ÷ new. 34.5 mm new, 40.0 mm now: 5.5 ÷ 34.5 = 15.9 %, over the 15 % limit: remove. Always divide by the new size, and use the maker's limit if it is stricter.

Worked exam questions

These questions are from the video. Try each one before you read the solution.

WORKED EXAMPLE 1

A hook's throat opening was 34.5 mm when new. It now measures 40.0 mm. How much has it opened, and with a 15 % removal limit, what do you do?

  • A. 5.5 %: keep using it
  • B. 15.9 %: remove it from service
  • C. 13.8 %: keep using it
  • D. 15.9 %: keep using it at a lower WLL

Answer: B. 15.9 %: remove it from service

40 minus 34.5 is 5.5 mm. Always divide by the new size. 5.5 over 34.5 is 15.9 %. Over 15.

Change = (40.0 − 34.5) ÷ 34.5 = 5.5 ÷ 34.5 = 15.9 %, over 15 %: remove from service (WorkSafeBC s. 15.29)

Remove it from service. It's B. A gives the millimetres, not the percent. C divides by the measured size. D doesn't exist. An opened hook isn't derated, it's scrapped.

WORKED EXAMPLE 2

3 t hook: throat 34.5 mm new, 39.0 mm today. Twist 4°, wear 4 %, no cracks. Two legs in the bowl at 80° included. The maker allows no throat increase. Can you use it?

  • A. Yes: every reading is inside the 15 %, 10° and 10 % limits
  • B. Yes, once the legs go on a master link
  • C. No: the throat has opened 13 %, and the maker allows no increase. Remove it
  • D. No: each leg is more than 45° from the centreline

Answer: C. No: the throat has opened 13 %, and the maker allows no increase. Remove it

Throat first. 39 minus 34.5 is 4.5. Over 34.5, 13 %. Under the 15 % rule, but the maker allows no increase. That hook has been overloaded. Twist and wear are fine. 80° is 40 on each side, also fine.

(39.0 − 34.5) ÷ 34.5 = 13.0 %: maker allows no increase → remove. Twist, wear, 40° each side: OK

Remove it. A stops at the regulation. B fixes a problem that isn't there. D forgets to split the included angle.

Common mistakes

  • Dividing the throat change by the measured size, not the new size
  • Passing a hook at 13 % without checking the maker's limit
  • Thinking a tip-loaded hook is fine because the load is under the stamped WLL
  • Mixing up 90° included with 45° on each side
  • Letting the latch carry load, or using a hook with a bent tip

Divide the throat change by the new size, check the maker's limit before passing a hook, never tip load, and remember 90° included is 45° each side.
