Wire rope inspection: broken wires, diameter and discard

A wire rope sling can be under its rated load and still fail, if it is past its discard limits. The rating on the tag is for a rope in good shape. The Red Seal exam expects you to read a rope's construction, count broken wires the right way, measure the diameter, and know the damage that takes a sling out of service.

What 6 × 19 IWRC means

The first number is the strands laid around the core: six. The second is the wires in each strand, as a class. The 6 × 19 class covers about 16 to 26 wires per strand; the 6 × 36 class covers 27 to 49. Size for size they have the same breaking strength. The 6 × 36 has more, finer wires, so it bends around a sheave more easily; the 6 × 19 has bigger outer wires that stand up to wear.

The letters are the core. IWRC is an independent wire rope core, a small steel rope in the middle. It is stronger than a fibre core and resists crushing. A fibre core is weaker, crushes more easily and can't take much heat.

Lay is the way the rope is twisted. In regular lay the wires in each strand turn opposite to the strands, so the outer wires run almost along the rope. In Lang lay the wires and strands turn the same way: it stands up to wear and bending better, but it crushes more easily and both ends must be held so it can't untwist. Right lay strands climb to the right.

What takes a wire rope sling out of service

Any one of these removes a wire rope sling:

  • Broken wires: 10 in one lay, or 5 in one strand in one lay
  • Kinks, crushing, birdcaging: any distortion
  • Heat damage (fibre core: over 200 °F / 93 °C)
  • Severe corrosion · outer wires worn by 1/3
  • Diameter loss past the limit · cracked or worn fittings

Inspect every sling before use, each day, along its whole length, both eyes and every fitting. Damage hides at the eyes, where the rope bends and rubs. If a sling fails, tag it out and remove it at once.

Counting broken wires in one lay

A rope lay is the length of rope it takes one strand to go once around. On a six-strand rope, follow one strand and count the strands as they come up: two, three, four, five, six. The next one is the first strand again. That is one lay.

Find the worst lay and count there. A sling comes out at 10 broken wires in one lay, or 5 in one strand in that lay. Eight broken wires with five in one strand is a remove: the strand limit is reached even though the total is under ten. Breaks in other lays don't add to it.

Running ropes on cranes and hoists have tighter limits in the British Columbia and Alberta rules: 6 broken wires in one lay, or 3 in one strand. Four broken with three in one strand passes as a sling but fails as a running rope.

Measuring the diameter

Measure across the crowns, the high points of opposite strands, not across the flats between strands. A new 3/4 in rope reads 0.750 in across the crowns, but only about 0.686 in across the flats. Read the flats and you would scrap a good rope. Take two readings at right angles and average them, and record the diameter when the rope goes into service; new rope often measures a little over its size.

The BC and Alberta rules allow 1/64 in (0.4 mm) on rope up to 5/16 in (8 mm), 3/64 in (1 mm) over that up to 3/4 in (19 mm), 1/16 in (2 mm) up to 1-1/8 in (29 mm) and 3/32 in (3 mm) above. A 3/4 in rope worn to 0.700 in has lost 0.050 in, about 6.7 %. That is past 3/64 in (0.047 in), so it comes out. A 5/8 in (15.9 mm) rope that measures 14.6 mm has lost about 1.3 mm, past the 1 mm limit.

Damage you can see

A kink is permanent: pulling it straight hides it but doesn't fix it. A birdcage is strands opened up like a cage, often after a shock load. Crushing flattens the rope. Heat shows as blue or straw coloured wires; a fibre core sling comes out above 200 °F (93 °C), and above 400 °F an IWRC sling follows the maker's rules. Light surface rust is cleaned, lubricated and watched; pitted wires come out.

The formula

Sling: out at 10 broken in one lay, or 5 in one strand
6 × 19 IWRC: 6 strands, about 19 wires each, steel core
Running rope: 6 and 3
Measure across the crowns
Kink, birdcage, crush, heat, pitting: out

Find the worst rope lay, then count. Ten broken wires in that lay, or five in one strand of it, removes a wire rope sling. A running rope on a crane comes out at six and three. Then measure across the crowns and compare the loss with the limit for that rope size.

Worked exam questions

These questions are from the video. Try each one before you read the solution.

WORKED EXAMPLE 1

A wire rope sling has 8 broken wires in its worst rope lay. 5 of them are in one strand. What do you do?

  • A. Keep using it: 8 is under the limit of 10
  • B. Keep using it and inspect it again next week
  • C. Remove it: 5 broken wires in one strand in one lay
  • D. Remove it only if the breaks are on the outside

Answer: C. Remove it: 5 broken wires in one strand in one lay

There are two limits, and either one takes the sling out. 10 in one lay, or five in one strand in one lay. 8 is under 10, but five in one strand is at the limit.

Sling limits: 10 in one lay OR 5 in one strand in one lay. Either one removes it.

A is the most common mistake: checking the total and forgetting the strand.

WORKED EXAMPLE 2

3/4 in 6 × 19 IWRC sling. Worst lay: 9 broken wires, 4 in one strand. Across the crowns: 0.705 in. Someone straightened a kink near the eye last week. What do you do?

  • A. Keep it: every count is under the limit
  • B. Keep it: 0.045 in lost is under 3/64 in
  • C. Remove it: the kink
  • D. Remove it: 9 broken wires is over the limit

Answer: C. Remove it: the kink

Check each limit. 9 is under 10. 4 is under five. 750 minus 705 is 45 thousandths, just under 360 fourths. Every number passes. But the kink doesn't. A kink is permanent damage to the rope's structure, and straightening it only hides it.

9 < 10 and 4 < 5 · 0.750 − 0.705 = 0.045 in < 3/64 in (0.047) · but a kink is permanent distortion → remove

A and B trusted the numbers and missed the kink. D read nine as over the limit. It isn't, but it's close. Watch that sling.

Common mistakes

  • Counting the whole rope, not one lay
  • Missing 5 in one strand
  • Using sling limits on a running rope
  • Measuring across the flats
  • Thinking a straightened kink is fixed

Count one lay at a time, check the strand limit as well as the total, and measure across the crowns.
