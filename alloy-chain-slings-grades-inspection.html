Alloy chain slings: grades, sling angles, heat, wear and stretch

A chain sling that hung by a furnace door for a shift can break at 80 % of its rating. Chain takes heat, abrasion and rough handling that would ruin a synthetic sling, but it hides damage well, and a link doesn't stretch much before it breaks. The Red Seal exam asks about grades, ratings, angles and inspection. Rules differ by province, so check yours.

Grades: only alloy chain lifts

The grade is stamped on the links. From the makers' tables, a single leg of 3/8 in chain is rated 7,100 lb in Grade 80, 8,800 lb in Grade 100 and 10,600 lb in Grade 120. For 1/2 in it's 12,000 lb in Grade 80, 15,000 lb in Grade 100 and 17,900 lb in Grade 120. Higher grade, stronger steel, more capacity for the same size and weight. Chain makers use a design factor of 4: a new leg breaks at about four times its rating. Some provincial rules ask for more on rigging.

Grade 43 high test chain and Grade 70 transport chain are not alloy and not for overhead lifting. BC's OHS Regulation (s. 15.47) bans proof coil and transport chain for hoisting, and Ontario's construction regulation (s. 176) allows only alloy chain or chain made for the purpose.

Legs and angles

Two legs of 3/8 in Grade 80 straight down are rated twice one leg, 14,200 lb. Spread the legs and each one pulls sideways as well as up, so the rating drops: one leg × two × the sine of the angle from horizontal. At 60° it's 12,300 lb, at 45° 10,000 lb, and at 30° 7,100 lb, the same as a single leg. At 30°, 10,000 lb of load puts 10,000 lb in each leg.

A four-leg sling is rated like a three-leg one, because on a stiff load one leg may carry almost nothing. Four legs of 1/2 in Grade 100 at 60° are good for 15,000 × 3 × 0.866, about 39,000 lb, and 30,000 lb on it puts about 11,547 lb in each loaded leg.

Heat: you can't see the damage

Heat changes the steel's heat treatment, and you can't see how much strength is gone. The makers say chain heated over 1,000 °F comes out of service, and BC (s. 15.51) keeps alloy chain under 260 °C unless the maker allows more. Below 1,000 °F the makers give a table. Up to 400 °F there is no change. Above that it depends on the grade: Grade 80 heated to 650 °F and cooled keeps a permanent 5 % loss in the Crosby and Lift-All tables, while Grade 100 at the same heat loses 15 %. Used while still hot, the loss is bigger again. The makers don't all agree on every row, so read the table for your chain, and if you don't know the temperature, take it out.

Never weld or heat treat alloy chain yourself. British Columbia, Alberta and Ontario all prohibit it.

Wear, damage and stretch

Links wear where they bear on each other, at the ends. Measure there with a caliper, across the wire, and compare with the maker's minimum. New 3/8 in chain is about 0.39 in; the minimum in the Crosby and Peerless tables is 0.342 in. Worn 14 %, it reads 0.339 in: under the minimum, out. There's no derating worn chain on the job. Nicks, gouges, cracks, bent or twisted links and weld spatter also take a sling out until a qualified person repairs it, or it's scrapped. BC's s. 15.48 lists the same damage.

Measure the reach from the bearing point of the master link to the bearing point of the hook and compare it with the tag. Columbus McKinnon says any stretch means the chain has been overloaded and it comes out of service. A leg that's grown 3 %, about 1-3/4 in on a 5 ft leg, or one leg longer than the others, means the whole sling is done.

The rules add more. In BC the tag shows the size, grade, working load limit, reach, number of legs and the maker; slings get a visual check before each shift, and chain slings a recorded thorough inspection at least once a year. On Ontario construction projects, alloy chain is checked at least weekly while in use. BC takes a hook out at 15 % throat opening or 10° of twist (s. 15.29).

The formula

Capacity = leg × legs × sin angle (max 3 legs)
Grade 80, 100, 120: alloy, for lifting
Over 1,000 °F: out · below: maker's table
Wear: caliper vs the maker's minimum
Any stretch: out

Capacity = one leg × the number of legs × the sine of the angle from horizontal, and never count more than three legs. Two legs of 1/2 in Grade 80 at 45°: 12,000 × 2 × 0.707 = 16,971 lb, which the maker's table rounds to 17,000 lb.

Worked exam questions

These questions are from the video. Try each one before you read the solution.

WORKED EXAMPLE 1

A 2-leg sling of 1/2 in Grade 80 chain (12,000 lb a leg) works with the legs at 45° from horizontal. What is its rated capacity?

  • A. 24,000 lb
  • B. 20,800 lb
  • C. 17,000 lb
  • D. 8,500 lb

Answer: C. 17,000 lb

One leg, times 2 legs, times the sine of 45, 0.707. That's 16,971. The maker's table rounds it to 17,000.

12,000 × 2 × sin 45° = 12,000 × 2 × 0.707 = 16,971 → 17,000 lb (maker's table)

A forgot the angle. B used 60°. D used only one leg.

WORKED EXAMPLE 2

A 3/8 in Grade 100 leg (8,800 lb) was heated to 700 °F, then cooled. Using the Crosby table (20 % permanent loss at 700 °F), what WLL do you use?

  • A. 8,800 lb
  • B. 7,040 lb
  • C. 5,280 lb
  • D. Remove it: it went over 400 °F

Answer: B. 7,040 lb

8,800, times 1 minus 0.2. 7,040 lb.

8,800 × (1 − 0.20) = 7,040 lb (Crosby Grade 100 table, after exposure; below 1,000 °F)

A ignores the heat. C used the bigger loss for chain used while still hot. D is too strict for this maker, whose limit is 1,000. But if you don't know the temperature, take it out.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting the leg angle
  • Counting all four legs
  • Trusting chain that looks fine after heat
  • No caliper at the bearing points
  • Lifting with transport or high test chain

Use the sine of the leg angle, rate a four-leg sling as three, read the maker's heat table, measure wear with a caliper, and never lift with Grade 43 or Grade 70 chain.
