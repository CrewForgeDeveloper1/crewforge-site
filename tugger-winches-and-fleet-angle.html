Tugger winches and fleet angle: lead sheave, layers, wraps and anchors

A tugger pulling a tenth of its rating can still throw its rope off the drum if the lead sheave is too close. The Red Seal exam asks you to work out the fleet angle, place the lead sheave, allow for the pull you lose as the drum fills, keep enough wraps on the drum, and find the load a snatch block puts on its anchor.

Fleet angle

The fleet angle is the angle between the rope at the drum flange and a straight line from the lead sheave to the middle of the drum. Its tangent is half the drum width divided by the distance to the lead sheave. A 24 in drum with the sheave 40 ft (480 in) away: 12 ÷ 480 = 0.025, so 1.43°.

North American winch makers give 1.5° for a smooth drum and 2° for a grooved drum. Too big, and the rope can't travel back across the drum: it piles up at the flange and can jump it. With the sheave only 10 ft away, the same drum runs at 5.71°. Too small, under about 0.5°, and the rope can pile in the middle. Keep the sheave on the drum's centre line: 6 in off centre at 40 ft, the angle to the far flange is 2.15°.

Where the lead sheave goes

Work it backwards: distance = half the drum width ÷ tan(limit). For the 24 in smooth drum, 12 ÷ tan 1.5° = 458 in, about 38 ft. The shop rule is about 19 drum widths for a smooth drum. A grooved drum allows 2°, so 12 ÷ tan 2° = 344 in, about 28.6 ft. An 18 in smooth drum needs about 28.6 ft.

Line pull drops as the drum fills

A tugger's rated pull is for the first layer, the bare drum. The drum turns with a fixed torque, and pull equals torque divided by radius. Each layer adds one rope diameter to the radius. With an 8 in barrel and 1/2 in rope, the first layer works at 4.25 in, the third at 5.25 in: 5,000 × 4.25 ÷ 5.25 = 4,048 lb. On the fourth layer it's about 3,696 lb, and on a full sixth layer about 3,148 lb, 37 % less. The line speeds up as the drum fills, but you lose pull.

Wraps on the drum

The rope's end is clamped to the drum, but the clamp isn't built to hold the load: friction on the wraps carries it. Winch makers want at least three full wraps left on the drum, some want five, and the B.C. hoisting rules set two as the minimum. With too few, the clamp sees most of the pull and the rope can pull off the drum.

Snatch blocks and anchors

A snatch block's anchor feels both parts of the line. Its load is 2 × line pull × cos(angle between the lines ÷ 2). With 1,600 lb of line pull: 90° gives 1.41 × 1,600 = 2,263 lb; 60° gives 1.73 × 1,600 = 2,771 lb; 0°, the line doubling back, gives twice the pull, 3,200 lb; 120° gives exactly the line pull.

Hang the block on the load and dead-end the rope to an anchor, and two parts share the pull: a load that needs 6,000 lb takes 3,000 lb of line pull, ignoring sheave friction, and the dead end holds 3,000 lb. The load moves at half the line speed. Never stand in the bight, the area inside the angle of the line: if an anchor or the rope fails, the line whips straight through it.

The formula

Fleet angle: tan α = (width ÷ 2) ÷ distance · 1.5° smooth, 2° grooved
Smooth drum: lead sheave about 19 × drum width away
Pull drops as layers build: P = P₁ × r₁ ÷ r
Keep the maker's minimum wraps (3 or more)
Block anchor = 2 × T × cos(angle ÷ 2) · stay out of the bight

Take half the drum width, divide by the distance to the lead sheave, and find the angle whose tangent that is. Keep it to 1.5° on a smooth drum and 2° on a grooved one.

Worked exam questions

These questions are from the video. Try each one before you read the solution.

WORKED EXAMPLE 1

A smooth-drum tugger's drum is 24 in wide between flanges. The lead sheave is 30 ft away, on the drum's centre line. What is the fleet angle, and is it acceptable?

  • A. 0.95°, acceptable
  • B. 1.91°, too big for a smooth drum
  • C. 1.91°, acceptable on any drum
  • D. 3.82°, too big for any drum

Answer: B. 1.91°, too big for a smooth drum

Half the drum width is 12 in. 30 ft is 360 in. 12 over 360, and the angle is 1.91°.

tan α = 12 in ÷ 360 in = 0.0333 → 1.91°: over the 1.5° smooth-drum limit (it would pass on a grooved drum, 2°)

That's over 1.5, so it's B: too big for a smooth drum. It would pass on a grooved drum. A uses the full width the wrong way. D uses the full width.

WORKED EXAMPLE 2

A tugger pulls 3,000 lb through a snatch block with 60° between the lead line and the load line. What load does the block's anchor see?

  • A. 3,000 lb
  • B. 4,243 lb
  • C. 5,196 lb
  • D. 6,000 lb

Answer: C. 5,196 lb

2 times the line pull, times the cosine of half the angle. 2 times 3,000 times the cosine of 30. Or use the factor, 1.73.

Anchor = 2 × T × cos(60° ÷ 2) = 2 × 3,000 × 0.866 = 5,196 lb (factor 1.73)

5,196 lb. C. A is just the line pull. B is the 90° factor. D is the factor at 0°.

WORKED EXAMPLE 3

You're hauling a 20,000 lb machine on skates (rolling resistance 0.02) up a 3° ramp. The line runs through a snatch block on a column, with 90° between the lines. About what load does the column's anchor see?

  • A. 566 lb
  • B. 1,446 lb
  • C. 2,045 lb
  • D. 2,892 lb

Answer: C. 2,045 lb

First the line pull. 0.02 times the cosine of 3°, plus the sine of 3°. That's 0.02 plus 0.0523. Times 20,000 is about 1,446 lb. Now the block: 90° gives a factor of 1.41.

Pull = 20,000 × (0.02 × cos 3° + sin 3°) = 20,000 × (0.0200 + 0.0523) = 1,446 lb; anchor = 1,446 × 1.414 = 2,045 lb

About 2,045 lb. It's C. A forgets the ramp. B forgets the block. D doubles the line pull, which is the 0° case.

Common mistakes

  • Using the full drum width in the fleet angle
  • Forgetting the rating is for the first layer
  • Running the rope down to its last wraps
  • Giving the block anchor only the line pull
  • Standing in the bight

Use half the drum width, plan for the layer you'll be on, keep the maker's minimum wraps, and rate the anchor for the vector sum, not the line pull.
