Fibre rope: types, knots and capacities

A 3/4 in manila rope has a minimum breaking strength of 4,860 lb. Tie a bowline in it and it breaks at about 3,402 lb. Millwrights use fibre rope for hand lines, tag lines, lashing and light hoisting, and the Red Seal exam asks how strong each fibre is, how much load a rope may carry and what a knot does to that number. Rules differ by province, so check yours.

Working load limit: breaking strength ÷ design factor

The maker's table gives a minimum breaking strength: the load where a new rope, tested with spliced eyes, breaks. You never work near it. Divide by a design factor. WorkSafeBC Table 15-1 sets 5 for synthetic fibre rope slings, and the Canadian Roofing Reference Manual uses 5 for hoisting and 10 for people. Alberta's OHS Code allows rigging that lifts a worker to carry no more than 10 % of its breaking strength.

For the 3/4 in manila rope: 4,860 ÷ 5 = 972 lb working load limit. Holding a worker: 4,860 ÷ 10 = 486 lb. The factor covers what the test rope never sees: wear, wet, knots, age and shock loads.

What a knot costs

Every knot bends the rope hard around itself, and the fibres on the outside of the bend take more than their share. Published efficiencies (Mazzella): eye splice about 100 %, figure eight 75–80 %, bowline 70–75 %, clove hitch 60–65 %, two half hitches 60–70 %, sheet bend 48–58 %, square knot 43–57 %. Plan on the low end. As a rule of thumb, a knot can cut a rope's strength by up to half.

Working load limit with a knot = breaking strength × knot efficiency ÷ design factor. A 5/8 in nylon rope (8,019 lb) with a bowline: 8,019 × 0.70 = 5,613 lb at the knot, ÷ 5 = 1,123 lb. The same manila rope as above with a square knot: 4,860 × 0.43 ÷ 5 = 418 lb, less than half of its 972 lb without the knot.

Which knot for which job

Bowline: a fixed loop that won't slip or close up, and unties after a heavy load. Clove hitch: a quick tie to a post or pipe, but it can work loose if the pull keeps changing direction, so finish it with half hitches. Round turn and two half hitches: tie off a line that stays under load. Sheet bend: join two ropes, even of different sizes. Square knot: binding only, for bundles and lashing; never join load lines with it.

Know your fibres

Same 1 in size, minimum breaking strength from the Atlantic Cordage 3-strand table: manila 8,100 lb, polypropylene 11,543 lb, polyester 17,798 lb, nylon 20,007 lb. Nylon is about 2.5 times manila. In metric, a 16 mm polyester rope is 3,195 kg; with a figure eight, 3,195 × 0.75 ÷ 5 = 479 kg.

Manila is a natural fibre: the weakest, and it rots and mildews if stored wet. In BC, natural fibre rope must not be used for hoisting with a powered hoist (OHS Regulation s. 15.8). Nylon stretches the most (up to 40 %), loses about 10 % of its strength wet and is attacked by acids. Polyester stretches about 5 % and is attacked by alkalis. Polypropylene floats (specific gravity 0.93), breaks down in sunlight and melts at about 165 °C (330 °F). Keep fibre rope slings below 82 °C (180 °F) and above −29 °C (−20 °F). A stretched rope stores energy, so never stand in line with a loaded rope.

Inspect before every use

Untwist the strands: the inside should be bright and clean, with no broken yarns and no powdery fibre. Remove the rope for cut or broken fibres, rot, mildew or discolouration, melted or charred spots, kinks, stiffness, or wear of 10 % of its diameter (CCOHS). Rate a rope on its most worn section, and never knot or repair a damaged rope to keep it in service.

The formula

Design factor 5 for loads, 10 for people
WLL = breaking strength × knot efficiency ÷ design factor
Splice 100 % · figure eight 75 · bowline 70 · clove 60 · sheet bend 48 · square 43
Nylon strongest and stretchiest · polyester low stretch · polypropylene floats · manila weakest
Inspect inside the strands; rate on the worst spot

Working load limit = minimum breaking strength × knot efficiency ÷ design factor (5 for loads, 10 for people). A 3/4 in manila rope with a bowline: 4,860 × 0.70 ÷ 5 = 680 lb.

Worked exam questions

These questions are from the video. Try each one before you read the solution.

WORKED EXAMPLE 1

A 5/8 in nylon rope has a minimum breaking strength of 8,019 lb. It is tied to the load with a bowline (70 % efficiency). Using a design factor of 5, what is the working load limit?

  • A. 1,604 lb
  • B. 1,123 lb
  • C. 5,613 lb
  • D. 2,291 lb

Answer: B. 1,123 lb

First the knot. 8,019 times 0.7 is about 5,613. That's where it breaks now. Then the design factor. 5,613 over 5.

8,019 × 0.70 = 5,613 lb at the knot; 5,613 ÷ 5 = 1,123 lb

About 1,123 lb. It's B. A forgot the knot. C forgot the design factor. D divided by the knot efficiency instead of multiplying.

WORKED EXAMPLE 2

Two 1/2 in polyester ropes (minimum breaking strength 4,577 lb each) are joined with a square knot (43 % efficiency) to lift a 500 lb load. Using a design factor of 5, what is the WLL with the knot, and can it make the lift?

  • A. 915 lb: yes
  • B. 1,968 lb: yes
  • C. 394 lb: no
  • D. 439 lb: yes

Answer: C. 394 lb: no

The knot first. 4,577 times 0.43 is about 1,968. Divide by 5. About 394. That's less than 500.

4,577 × 0.43 = 1,968 lb at the knot; 1,968 ÷ 5 = 394 lb < 500 lb: no (a sheet bend gives 439 lb, still no)

It can't make the lift. A forgot the knot. B forgot the design factor. And D is a sheet bend, 439, which still isn't enough. One rope long enough, with a spliced eye, is good for nine fifteen.

Common mistakes

  • Working at the breaking strength instead of the WLL
  • Forgetting the knot, or dividing by its efficiency
  • Using 5 instead of 10 when the rope holds a person
  • Joining load lines with a square knot
  • Manila on a powered hoist; nylon judged dry when wet

Work at the WLL, not the breaking strength; multiply by the knot efficiency; use 10 for people; never join load lines with a square knot.
