Wire rope terminations: clips, wedge sockets and efficiency

A wire rope is only as strong as its end. Two clips put on backwards on a half inch rope can let the eye pull out under a 4,000 lb load, even though a properly made eye on that rope is rated for more. Millwrights make and inspect terminations, and the Red Seal exam asks how to install them and how strong they are. Many rules don't allow clips to form the eyes of slings, so check your province's rules.

Clips: follow the maker's table

Take the number of clips, the turn-back and the torque from the maker's table for your rope size. From the Crosby table: 1/2 in rope takes 3 clips, 11-1/2 in of turn-back and 65 ft·lb; 5/8 in takes 3 clips, 12 in and 95 ft·lb; 3/4 in takes 4 clips, 18 in and 130 ft·lb; 1 in takes 5 clips, 26 in and 225 ft·lb. In metric, 11-1/2 in is 292 mm and 65 ft·lb is about 88 N·m.

The saddle goes on the live end, the part that takes the load. The U-bolt goes on the dead end. Never saddle a dead horse: a U-bolt on the live end crushes the wires that carry the load. Turn the rope back around a thimble; without one the eye can lose up to 10 % of its strength. If a pulley replaces the thimble, add a clip, and don't reuse clips.

Installing and re-torquing

Turn back the specified length around the thimble. Put the first clip one base width from the dead end and torque it. Put the next clip as close to the thimble as you can, snug but not tight. Space any others evenly between them, take up the slack, then torque every nut, going back and forth from one nut to the other.

Then apply the first load, at least as heavy as the loads it will see. The rope stretches and gets a little smaller, so the clips loosen: check the torque again and tighten, and keep checking it at every inspection. Some trade references give a clip spacing of about six rope diameters; the maker's method sets the spacing from the turn-back.

How strong is each end?

Efficiency is the strength of the termination as a percentage of the rope's catalogue breaking strength. Crosby clips are 80 % up to 7/8 in and 90 % from 1 in up. A wedge socket is 80 %. A pressed sleeve on a flemish eye is 95 % up to 1 in. A hand-tucked splice runs from about 90 % on small rope to 80 % on 7/8 in and larger. Swaged and spelter sockets are 100 %.

On a 1 in rope with a breaking strength of 103,400 lb, a clip eye is good for 103,400 × 0.90 = 93,060 lb. Divide by the design factor of 5 for the working load: 18,612 lb. A half inch rope (26,600 lb) with clips gives 21,280 lb, and 4,256 lb working load.

Wedge sockets

The live line must come out of the socket straight, in line with the pin. Put the rope in backwards, with the live line on the wedge side, and it bends sharply as it leaves. For standard 6- to 8-strand rope the tail is at least 6 rope diameters and never less than 6 in; rotation-resistant rope needs 20 diameters. On 3/4 in rope, 6 diameters is 4.5 in, so the 6 in minimum governs.

Secure the tail by clipping it to a short piece of rope, or by looping it back with one clip. Never clip the tail to the live line: that changes how the socket loads and puts extra stress on the rope that carries the load.

Inspecting swaged and pressed fittings

Look for cracks in the fitting, rope that has slipped out of the sleeve or socket, and broken wires where the rope enters the fitting, where the bending is worst. Any of those takes it out of service.

The formula

Clips 80 / 90 % · wedge 80 % · swaged, spelter 100 %
Saddle on the live end
Clips, turn-back, torque: from the maker's table
Re-torque after the first load
Wedge: live line in line with the pin · tail 6 d, at least 6 in

Termination strength = the rope's minimum breaking strength × the efficiency of the end. The working load limit of the eye = that strength ÷ the design factor of 5. On 1 in rope with clips: 103,400 × 0.90 = 93,060 lb, ÷ 5 = 18,612 lb.

Worked exam questions

These questions are from the video. Try each one before you read the solution.

WORKED EXAMPLE 1

A 1 in wire rope has a minimum breaking strength of 103,400 lb. What is the strength of a properly made Crosby clip eye?

  • A. 103,400 lb
  • B. 82,720 lb
  • C. 20,680 lb
  • D. 93,060 lb

Answer: D. 93,060 lb

Crosby rates clips at 90 % on 1 in rope and larger. 103,400, times 0.9. 93,060 lb.

Crosby clips: 90 % for 1 in and larger → 103,400 × 0.90 = 93,060 lb

B used 80 %, the figure for smaller rope. C divided the full strength by 5. That's a working load, and it skipped the efficiency.

WORKED EXAMPLE 2

A 1 in wire rope, minimum breaking strength 103,400 lb, with a Crosby clip eye. How many clips, how much turn-back, and what is the working load limit of the eye (design factor 5)?

  • A. 4 clips, 19 in, 16,544 lb
  • B. 5 clips, 26 in, 20,680 lb
  • C. 5 clips, 26 in, 18,612 lb
  • D. 6 clips, 34 in, 18,612 lb

Answer: C. 5 clips, 26 in, 18,612 lb

The 1 in row says five clips and 26 in turned back. Clips on 1 in rope are 90 %. 103,400, times 0.9, is 93,060. Divided by 5, 18,612 lb.

1 in row: 5 clips, 26 in · 103,400 × 0.90 = 93,060 lb · ÷ 5 = 18,612 lb

A used the seven eighths row and 80 %. B forgot the efficiency. D read the next row down.

Common mistakes

  • U-bolts on the live end
  • Too few clips for the rope size
  • No re-torque after the first load
  • Wedge tail under 6 in, or clipped to the live line
  • Assuming every end is 100 %

Saddle on the live end, clips and torque from the maker's table, re-torque after the first load, and a wedge tail of at least 6 in.
