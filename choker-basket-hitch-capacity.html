Choker and basket hitch capacity: how the hitch changes a sling's rating

The tag on a sling gives its rating in a straight vertical pull, but slings are rarely used that way. Choke it around the load and you lose capacity. Basket it and you can double it, or not, depending on the angle and what it bends around.

One sling, four hitches

Take one wire rope sling rated 8,000 lb. In a vertical hitch, one leg straight up, it carries its full rating: 8,000 lb. Choke it around the load and the sling bends sharply around itself at the eye; a wire rope choker is good for 75 %, or 6,000 lb. In a basket with both legs straight up, two legs share the load and it can carry twice the vertical rating: 16,000 lb. Bring the legs in to 60° and the basket drops to about 13,850 lb, because the sling angle factor applies just as it does on a bridle.

  • Vertical: 100 % of the tag rating
  • Choker: 75 % wire rope · 80 % chain, synthetic (choke ≥ 120°)
  • Basket, vertical legs: 200 % (wire rope D/d ≥ 25 : 1)
  • Angled legs: ÷ load angle factor (60° → 1.155)

Always check the sling tag: it lists the rating for each hitch.

The choke angle

The choker rating assumes a choke angle of 120° or more. Let the choke form naturally, at about 135°, and you keep the full choker rating, 6,000 lb for our sling. Force the choke down to 100° and the table takes it to 87 %, or 5,220 lb. Push it to 75° and it is 74 %, or 4,440 lb. Never force the choke down by beating it with a hammer; let it seat under load. Both reductions apply together: a wire rope sling rated 6,000 lb vertical, choked at 75°, is good for 6,000 × 0.75 × 0.74 = 3,330 lb. This angle-of-choke table is for wire rope; for synthetic and chain chokers under 120°, use the sling maker's chart.

Baskets and the D/d factor

Wire rope basket ratings assume the sling bends around something at least 25 times its own diameter. A ¾ in rope basketed around a 24 in drum is 32 : 1, so a 10,000 lb load with the legs at 60° keeps the full rating, about 13,850 lb. Put the same basket around a 9 in pipe and the ratio is 12 : 1. That isn't on the table, so use the next lower row, 10 : 1, which allows 86 % of the basket rating: about 11,913 lb. Wire rope needs a big bend. Synthetic slings and chain aren't rated this way; check their tags.

  • Basket = 2 × WLL ÷ LAF × D/d factor
  • Wire rope: D/d ≥ 25 : 1 → 1.00
  • 20 : 1 0.92 · 15 : 1 0.88 · 8 : 1 0.84 · 1 : 1 0.50

Keep the load from sliding: a basket on a smooth round load can roll out.

Picking the hitch

  • Vertical: one lifting point, load won't turn
  • Choker: bundles, long round loads (double wrap holds best)
  • Basket: only if the load can't slide or roll out
  • Bridle: two or more lifting points
  • Sharp edges: softeners or edge guards

A double wrap choker goes around the load twice, holds all the way round and draws the pieces of a bundle together, so it is the hitch for bundles and long round loads. On a long bundle, use two of them.

Knots riggers use

  • Bowline: fixed loop, won't slip, unties easily
  • Clove hitch: quick tie to a pipe or post; can slip
  • Round turn and two half hitches: tie off under load
  • Timber hitch: drag a log, pole or pipe
  • Sheet bend: join two ropes, even of different sizes

The bowline makes a fixed loop that won't slip and unties easily after a load. A clove hitch ties a line quickly to a pipe or post, but it can slip if the pull keeps changing. A round turn and two half hitches ties off a line that stays under load. One rule for all of them: a knot weakens a rope, and you never tie a knot in a sling to shorten it.

Tag lines and load control

Tag lines keep a load from swinging and turning as it moves. Without them, a turning load can pin someone against a column or catch a leg on the way past. Attach the tag lines before the lift. Stand clear, never under the load. Never wrap a tag line around your hand or body. And keep them on until the load has landed.

The formula

Basket = 2 × WLL ÷ LAF × D/d factor
Wire rope: D/d ≥ 25 : 1 → 1.00
20 : 1 0.92 · 15 : 1 0.88 · 8 : 1 0.84 · 1 : 1 0.50
Keep the load from sliding or rolling out

A basket's capacity is twice the vertical rating, divided by the load angle factor for the leg angle. For wire rope, multiply by the D/d factor for the diameter it bends around: 25 : 1 or more keeps the full rating. A choker is the vertical rating times the choker factor (75 % wire rope, 80 % chain and synthetic), times the choke-angle percentage when a wire rope choke is under 120° (synthetic and chain: the maker's chart).

Worked exam questions

These questions are from the video. Try each one before you read the solution.

WORKED EXAMPLE 1

A wire rope sling is rated 6,000 lb vertical. It is used in a choker with a 75° choke angle. Choker = 75 %; 60–89° choke = 74 %. What is its rating?

  • A. 3,330 lb
  • B. 4,440 lb
  • C. 4,500 lb
  • D. 6,000 lb

Answer: A. 3,330 lb

Both factors apply. 6,000 times 0.75 is 4,500. Times 0.74 is 3,330.

6,000 × 0.75 × 0.74 = 3,330 lb

C stopped after the first factor. B used only the second.

WORKED EXAMPLE 2

A ¾ in wire rope sling rated 8,000 lb vertical is used in a basket around a 6 in pipe, legs at 60° (LAF 1.155). Basket D/d factor at 8 : 1 = 0.84. What is the capacity?

  • A. 16,000 lb
  • B. 13,853 lb
  • C. 11,636 lb
  • D. 6,000 lb

Answer: C. 11,636 lb

Three steps. 2 times 8,000 is 16,000. Divide by 1.155 for the angle. About 13,853. Now the bend. 6 divided by ¾ is 8 : 1. Times 0.84. About 11,636.

D/d = 6 ÷ 0.75 = 8 → 0.84; 2 × 8,000 ÷ 1.155 × 0.84 = 11,636 lb

A forgot the angle and the bend. B forgot the bend. D is a choker.

Common mistakes

  • Vertical rating used for a choker
  • Sharp choke angle: second reduction missed
  • Basket doubled but the angle forgotten
  • Wire rope basket: D/d factor missed
  • Tag lines off before the load lands

Rate a choker as a choker, apply every reduction that the hitch calls for, and keep the tag lines on until the load lands.
