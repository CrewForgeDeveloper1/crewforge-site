Cribbing, jacking and blocking: contact area, crushing and height

Set the edge of a twenty ton machine on a crib of four by fours, two per layer, and the wood crushes. Millwrights crib every time they jack a machine, set a base or change a bearing on a heavy shaft. The Red Seal exam asks how much a crib can carry, how tall it can be, and how you crib as you lift. Rules differ by province, so check yours.

Count the contact points

Cribbing is timber stacked in crossed layers under a load. Blocking is the solid timber you set under a raised machine so nobody depends on a jack or a hoist to hold it. Each layer crosses the one below at a right angle, and the load only goes down where one timber crosses another. Those crossings are the contact points: the timbers per layer, squared. Two on two makes four; three on three makes nine.

Use the actual size, not the nominal one. A 4 × 4 is really 3.5 in square, so each contact point is 12.25 in². Four points give 49 in². Bearing is the load over the contact area: 10,000 lb on 49 in² is 204 psi.

Plan at 500 psi, know where it crushes

For planning, rigging and rescue shoring guides use about 500 psi on each contact point of softwood. The two-per-layer 4 × 4 crib is good for 49 × 500 = 24,500 lb. The wood itself gives up higher. CSA O86, the Canadian timber design standard, gives spruce-pine-fir a specified strength across the grain of 5.3 MPa, about 770 psi, so the same crib starts to crush near 37,700 lb. Douglas fir is stronger across the grain, 7 MPa, almost 50,000 lb for the same crib.

Put a 40,000 lb machine edge on that crib and the fibres crush. The fix is more wood touching wood. Three per layer gives nine points and 110.25 in², so 40,000 lb bears at 363 psi, under 500, and the planning capacity is 55,125 lb. Or go to 6 × 6 timbers (5.5 in actual): two per layer give 121 in² and 60,500 lb.

Height: no more than twice the width

The usual rigging rule keeps a crib no taller than twice its width. A Canadian contractor's cribbing procedure says the same, and uses the smaller width if the crib isn't square. Rescue crews allow up to three to one, but for moving machinery stick to two. With 4 × 4 timbers 24 in long, 13 layers is 45.5 in; one more layer is 49 in and over the limit.

Height matters because cribs get pushed sideways by a shifting load or a leaning jack. The push times the height tries to tip the crib over its far bottom edge; the load times half the width holds it down. So a crib tips when the side push, as a share of the load, is more than the width over twice the height. At the two to one limit that is always 25 %. Build it almost three to one and a push of only 17 % of the load tips it.

Crib as you go

You never leave a load sitting on a jack. The jack stands plumb on firm blocking under the jacking bracket. Lift about an inch, with thin packing tapped in tight to follow the load, so the gap never grows past that inch. When there is room for a new layer, slide it in, lower the load onto the crib and let the jack go slack. Rescue crews call it lift an inch, crib an inch: if the jack slips, the load drops an inch, not a foot. In Ontario, the industrial regulation (Reg. 851, s. 74) says raised machinery that someone could get under must be securely blocked.

Run the top layer across the edge of the load: an edge along the grain acts like a wedge and can split the timber. Use one kind of wood; mixed hardwoods carry only about 70 % of an all-oak crib. Let the corners overlap about 4 in past each crossing. Build on firm, level ground, and on soft ground start on a steel plate at least 1 in thick or a full first layer. If a crib starts making noise, replace it.

The formula

Contact points = (timbers per layer)²; bearing = load ÷ contact area
Plan about 500 psi on softwood; S-P-F crushes near 5.3 MPa (≈ 770 psi)
Height ≤ 2 × width; tips when side push ÷ load > W ÷ 2H
Crib as you go: short lifts, packing follows, lower onto the crib
Top layer across the edge; one species; firm base

Count the crossings, not the timbers: two per layer is four contact points. For 4 × 4s (3.5 in actual) that's 4 × 12.25 = 49 in². Divide the load by the area for the bearing, and multiply the area by 500 psi for the planning capacity: 24,500 lb.

Worked exam questions

These questions are from the video. Try each one before you read the solution.

WORKED EXAMPLE 1

A crib is built of 6 × 6 timbers (5.5 in actual), two per layer. Using 500 psi on each contact point, what load can it carry?

  • A. 15,125 lb
  • B. 60,500 lb
  • C. 24,500 lb
  • D. 72,000 lb

Answer: B. 60,500 lb

Two on two makes four contact points. Each is 5.5 by 5.5, 30.25 in². 4 of them is 121. Times 500.

4 contact points × 5.5 × 5.5 in = 121 in²; 121 × 500 psi = 60,500 lb

60,500 lb. It's B. A counts only one point. C is the 4 by 4 crib. D uses 6 by 6 nominal, not the real size.

WORKED EXAMPLE 2

A crib is 24 in wide and 48 in tall. About what side push at the top, as a share of the load on it, will tip it over?

  • A. 50 %
  • B. 25 %
  • C. 12.5 %
  • D. 200 %

Answer: B. 25 %

It tips when the push times the height beats the load times half the width. So the push over the load equals the width over twice the height. 24 over 96.

Tips when push × height > load × width ÷ 2 → push ÷ load > 24 ÷ (2 × 48) = 0.25

A quarter, 25 %. B. And at the 2 : 1 limit, that's always 25 %. A forgets the two. D turns it upside down.

Common mistakes

  • Using nominal size (6 × 6) instead of actual (5.5 × 5.5)
  • Counting timbers instead of contact points
  • Building taller than 2 × the width
  • Leaving the load on the jack
  • Laying the load's edge along the grain

Use the actual timber size, count contact points, round the number of layers down, and never leave the load on the jack while you work.
