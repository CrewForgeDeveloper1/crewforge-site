Exam guide · Red Seal Industrial Mechanic (Millwright)

The Red Seal millwright exam: how it works and how to study for it

A lot of good millwrights walk into the Red Seal exam without knowing how the questions are built or where the marks are. This guide covers the format, where the questions come from, how many each area gets, the three question types, and how to study and manage your time.

Video released 7 November 2026 · 15 min · Watch on YouTube · AI narration

What the exam is

The Red Seal Industrial Mechanic (Millwright) exam has 135 multiple-choice questions. Each has four options and exactly one correct answer, and every question is worth one mark. You get four hours, and the pass mark is 70 %, which is about 95 questions right.

It tests the trade at the journeyperson level, the way it is done across Canada, not just in your shop or your province. It is built on the Red Seal Occupational Standard (RSOS), not on a textbook, which is the biggest difference from the level exams you wrote in school. Red Seal says plainly that there are no trick questions, but the wrong answers are not always obvious.

Who writes it

Apprentices write it at the end of their apprenticeship; exactly when depends on the province. The other route is the trade qualifier, sometimes called a challenger: someone with years in the trade who never went through an apprenticeship. Trade qualifiers have to document their hours and cover the full scope of the trade. As one example, Manitoba asks a trade qualifier for 9,600 hours, about six years.

Every province and territory sets its own rules for the hours, the paperwork, the fees and what you bring on the day. Before anything else, contact your apprenticeship office and ask what they need from you.

Where the questions come from

The RSOS breaks the trade into six Major Work Activities (MWA), lettered A to F. Each is broken into tasks, and each task into sub-tasks. Every exam question ties back to a sub-task. A sub-task is only testable if journeypersons do it in at least 70 % of provinces and territories; that is called common core. If it is not common core, it is not on the exam. Tradespeople from across Canada write and review the questions, and decide how many each task gets.

Mechanical power transmission (32) and material handling (24) together are 56 questions, more than four out of every ten marks. A new RSOS has been published for this trade and exams based on it are being developed, so check the official exam page on red-seal.ca before you write.

Rigging is two tasks, worth 9 and 8: 17 questions between them. At the other end, robotics and automated equipment has one question. Spend your time roughly in proportion to the questions, but don't skip anything: half the exam, 67 questions, sits in tasks worth five questions or fewer.

Red Seal has a free self-assessment for each trade. You rate yourself on every task and nobody sees the results but you. Do it first; it shows you where your gaps are.

Three kinds of questions

TypeWhat it testsShare
Knowledge and recallFacts, terms, principles30–40 %
Procedural and applicationSteps, order, calculations40–50 %
Critical thinkingCauses, results, best choice20–30 %

Shares for this trade, from the Red Seal exam information page.

Knowledge and recall tests facts and terms. Procedural and application tests the steps, their order and the calculations, and it is the biggest group. Critical thinking gives you a symptom and asks for the cause, or a situation and asks for the best thing to do. Most of the exam is about doing the work, not reciting it.

How the questions are built

  • A short scenario, then the question
  • Key words: first, next, best, most likely, NOT
  • Wrong answers come from real mistakes
  • Units and angles can be given any way
  • Formula sheet and acronym list provided

Most questions start with a short scenario (a pump, a gearbox, a lift), then the question. Watch for the key words: first, next, best, most likely, and not, which flips the whole question. The wrong answers are not random: each one is the answer you would get from a common mistake, such as a forgotten step, a flipped ratio or the wrong angle. Numbers can come in pounds or kilograms, inches or millimetres, and angles can be measured from either side.

A formula sheet and a list of acronyms are given to you in the room. They are posted on the Red Seal website, so study from the same sheet. Knowing the formula isn't the hard part; knowing which one to use is.

How to study

  • Self-assessment first, then plan by weight
  • Short sessions, most days, over weeks
  • Test yourself; don't just reread
  • Learn why each wrong answer is wrong
  • Read the RSOS: terms, tools, glossary

Start with the self-assessment, then build a plan that follows the question counts. Study a little, most days, for weeks, not all weekend the week before: spreading practice out is one of the best-supported study methods there is. Test yourself rather than rereading notes. When you check answers, go through every option: why the right one is right and why each wrong one is wrong. Read the RSOS itself, because the exam uses its terms, not slang or brand names. Red Seal also warns that anyone selling the real exam questions is lying or breaking the law.

Calculator check

If you need a calculator, one is provided in the room; you can't bring your own, your phone or a smart watch. Before you start, key in sin 30°. You should get 0.5. If you get anything else, it isn't in degree mode, and every angle question will be wrong. Estimate before you calculate, convert to one set of units before you compare, and use sanity checks: a load angle factor is never less than 1.

Managing four hours

240 minutes over 135 questions is about a minute and three-quarters each. Some take 20 seconds and some take five minutes; it evens out. Read the stem twice, find the key word, and try to answer before you look at the options. If a question is eating your time, mark it, skip it on the bubble sheet too, and come back. At the two-hour mark you want to be about halfway, around 68 questions.

Don't leave anything blank. A guess gives you a one-in-four chance, and crossing off two wrong answers makes it one in two. Leave time at the end to check that every answer is on the right line of the bubble sheet.

If you don't pass

You can write again. Each province and territory sets its own rewrite rules. In Newfoundland and Labrador, for example, you wait at least 30 days, and before a third attempt you have to complete an approved course of study. You will usually get your results broken down by section: find the blocks where you lost the most marks, build your next plan around them, and go again.

Practise the question types

Three questions from the video, one of each kind of trap. Try each before you read the solution.

EXAMPLE 1

An 8,000 lb gearbox is lifted with a two-leg bridle. Each leg is 60° from vertical. What is the tension in each leg?

  • A. 4,000 lb
  • B. 4,620 lb
  • C. 8,000 lb
  • D. 16,000 lb

Answer: C. 8,000 lb

Convert first. 90 minus 60 is 30° from horizontal. The factor for 30 is 2. Half the load is 4,000 lb. 4,000 times 2 is 8,000 lb in each leg.

60° from vertical → 90° − 60° = 30° from horizontal → (8,000 ÷ 2) × 2.000 = 8,000 lb

Now look at the wrong ones. A forgot the angle factor. B used 60 as if it were from horizontal. D forgot to divide by 2 legs. Every wrong answer is a real mistake. And 30° from horizontal is the practical minimum, so on the job this rig needs a second look.

EXAMPLE 2

A millwright has applied a personal lock to the disconnect for a pump motor. What must be done before the coupling guard is removed?

  • A. Tell the operator the pump is down
  • B. Verify the motor cannot be started
  • C. Hang a tag on the coupling guard
  • D. Release the coupling bolts

Answer: B. Verify the motor cannot be started

The lock is on. But a lock on the wrong disconnect protects nobody. Before you start work, you verify that the energy is really isolated. On a motor, that usually means trying to start it at the local control, then putting the control back to off. Check for stored energy too.

Lock applied → verify zero energy (try to start at the local control, then return it to off) → then start work

A is a real step, but it comes before the lockout, not after. C doesn't isolate anything. And D is the job itself. When a question says before, or first, every option might be a real step. You're picking the one that comes next.

EXAMPLE 3

Which statement about setting up a portable extension ladder is NOT correct?

  • A. Set the base 1 ft out for every 4 ft up
  • B. Extend it at least 1 m (3 ft) above the landing
  • C. Set the base 1 ft out for every 2 ft up
  • D. Face the ladder when climbing

Answer: C. Set the base 1 ft out for every 2 ft up

3 of these are good practice. 1 ft out for every 4 ft up. That's the 4 : 1 rule. Extend it at least 1 m, or 3 ft, above the landing. And face the ladder when you climb.

The 4-to-1 rule: 1 ft (0.3 m) out for every 4 ft (1.2 m) up. One out for every two is far too flat.

One out for every two up puts the base much too far out, and the foot of the ladder can slip. The trap here is the word not. If you read too fast, you'll pick A, because it's the first true statement you see.
